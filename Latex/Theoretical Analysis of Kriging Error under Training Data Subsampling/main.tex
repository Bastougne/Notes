\begin{abstract}
    Gaussian process regression, or kriging, provides an optimal linear estimator under Gaussian prior assumptions. However, its high computational cost limits its applicability to large datasets. A common workaround involves subsampling the training data, typically by retaining only points near the estimation location.

    This induces a loss of accuracy whose impact is often left unquantified. In this paper, we derive explicit bounds on the \textbf{universal} kriging error introduced by such data reduction strategies. By expressing the variance and interpolation errors in terms of residual covariances and observations, \textbf{we show that this error is at most the simple kriging error plus the loss incurred on the estimation of the trend, which persists even when the discarded data are uncorrelated with the retained ones. This yields an upper bound computable from the retained data and the cross covariances only, and a nested decomposition that brackets the error between two computable quantities.} These results bridge the gap between heuristic subsampling strategies and rigorous performance guarantees, providing computable criteria for robust subsampled Gaussian process regression.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Gaussian process regression (GPR) \cite{bib:Rasmussen-regression} is a non-parametric Bayesian method widely used in spatial statistics such as astrophysics and geostatistics, and surrogate modelling. It is valued for its ability to provide not only point estimates but also uncertainty quantifications. However, its computational cost scales cubically with the number of training points, making it prohibitive in large-scale or real-time applications.

A substantial body of work has addressed this limitation through approximation techniques. Low-rank methods, such as the Nyström approximation \cite{bib:Nystrom} or the Karhunen–Loève decomposition \cite{bib:Karhunen_Loeve} can be used to reduce the computational cost. Sparse variational methods can also approximate the covariance structure by using pseudo-inputs \cite{bib:variational_sparse_GP} or spectral expansions \cite{bib:sparse_GP}. While these methods are effective, they typically alter the underlying Gaussian process model, potentially affecting its interpretability or calibration.

An alternative strategy is to reduce complexity by directly subsampling the training data. Local selections only consider the closest points to the prediction location \cite{bib:local_subsampling, bib:AK-PF-clustered}, while other methods include sub-modularity \cite{bib:observation_selection}, greedy heuristics \cite{bib:greedy_selection}, entropy-based criteria \cite{bib:GP_optimisation}, determinantal point processes \cite{bib:determinantal}, or nearest-neighbour techniques \cite{bib:nn_GP}. While such approaches are computationally appealing and preserve the original model assumptions, they usually lack rigorous guarantees on the error induced by discarding observations. Some theoretical efforts have examined error bounds for GPR itself \cite{bib:GPR_error_bounds}, low-rank approximations \cite{bib:bach_kernel} or random subsampling \cite{bib:random_selection}, but general bounds quantifying the training data subsampling remain scarce.

In this work, we provide explicit theoretical error bounds for subsampled GPR. Unlike previous methods that alter the model or rely on heuristic selection, our analysis focuses directly on the Gaussian process structure and yields interpretable, computable metrics to assess the impact of subsampling. In \cref{sec:problem_statement}, we recall the standard setting of \textbf{universal kriging for a vector-valued field, which estimates the unknown trend of the process from the data, ordinary kriging being the special case of a constant mean,} and introduce error metrics for subsampling. \Cref{sec:data_selection} reformulates both \textbf{the error covariance and the interpolation error} in terms of residual quantities, representing the information contained in the training data that is lost during subsampling. This leads to closed-form expressions that characterise the loss of information from discarding data. Building on these results, \cref{sec:error_bounds} derives \textbf{upper bounds that avoid inverting any matrix of the size of the discarded data, a nested decomposition that brackets the error, and bounds relying on entrywise assumptions on the kernel}. Finally, proofs and relevant calculations are provided in \cref{sec:appendix}. Together, these results clarify how the robustness to subsampling depends on \textbf{the correlation between retained and discarded data, the estimation of the trend,} and the observation noise regime.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem_statement}

\subsection{Gaussian Process Regression}\label{sec:gaussian_process_regression}

\textbf{[Manuscrit : renvoi à th:universal\_kriging et à th:kernel\_SPSD.] The field is the} $d_z$\textbf{-dimensional Gaussian process} $f$ \textbf{of mean} $x\mapsto g(x)\beta$ \textbf{and SPD matrix kernel} $k$\textbf{, observed at} $\tilde{N}$ \textbf{training points} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ \textbf{through} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}$\textbf{, with a noise of covariance} $\tilde{R}$\textbf{, and estimated at a query point} $x$ \textbf{where the quantity of interest is} $z\triangleq f(x)+\varepsilon$\textbf{, of query noise covariance} $R$\textbf{. We write} $\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)$ \textbf{for the universal kriging estimator of} $z$, $R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},g)$ \textbf{for its error covariance,} $\hat{m}_{\mathrm{UK}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)$ \textbf{for the estimated trend coefficients and} $M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\triangleq\left(g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^\top G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{-1}g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right)^{-1}$ \textbf{for their covariance.}

\textbf{This section rests on the bordered form of the kriging equations. Let} $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ \textbf{be the Gram matrix of the observations and} $\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ \textbf{its bordered counterpart, and let} $\bar{k}$ \textbf{and} $\bar{\mathbf{z}}$ \textbf{be the covariance and observation matrices bordered accordingly:}
\begin{equation}
    G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\triangleq k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})+I_{\tilde{N}}\otimes\tilde{R},\quad\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})&g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\\g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^\top&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)\\g(x)^\top\end{bmatrix},\quad\bar{\mathbf{z}}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\\0\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
\textbf{with} $\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\triangleq\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)^\top$\textbf{. Handling the unbiasedness constraints with Lagrange multipliers, the optimum is reached for:}
\begin{equation}
    \hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)=\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{z}}\label{eq:kriging_f}
\end{equation}

The associated error \textbf{covariance} is:
\begin{equation}
    R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},g)\triangleq\mathbb{V}\left[\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)-z\right]=k(x,x)+R-\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{-1}\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)\label{eq:kriging_R}
\end{equation}

A major limitation of this method is the $\mathcal{O}(\tilde{N}^3d_z^3)$ computational cost, stemming from the inversion of the Gram matrix $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$, \textbf{or equivalently of} $\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$, in both \eqref{eq:kriging_f} and \eqref{eq:kriging_R}. One common approach to mitigate this is to omit certain elements of $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ to reduce the size of $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$. However, this approximation introduces a loss of accuracy in \textbf{\eqref{eq:kriging_f}}. This paper aims to quantify the error induced by such subsampling approximations.

\subsection{Subsampling Error Metrics}\label{sec:subsampling_errors}

Formally, subsampling is achieved by splitting $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ into $\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r&\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d\end{bmatrix}^\top$, with $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$ being the $\tilde{N}^r$ retained data, usually close to the considered kriging location $x\in\mathcal{U}$, and $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d$ being the $\tilde{N}^d$ discarded data, usually far from $x$. A similar operation is performed on $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}$. This decomposition provides the foundation for all subsequent error bounds, as it explicitly isolates the predictive contribution of the discarded points from that of the retained training set.

Two error metrics between $\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)$ and $\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)$ are considered in this paper, each capturing a distinct aspect of the kriging approximation. The first metric corresponds to \textbf{the covariance of their difference, of which \Cref{def:L2_error} also retains the largest standard deviation over the directions of} $\mathbb{R}^{d_z}$. In contrast, since the kriging estimator is a deterministic function conditionally to $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}$, a natural alternative is to measure the \textbf{distance} between the two estimators, as proposed by \Cref{def:L1_error}.

\begin{definition}
    \textbf{Let} $E_r$ \textbf{denote the covariance of the difference between the full and subsampled estimators, and} $e_r$ \textbf{the largest error it induces in the} $L^2$ \textbf{space over the unit directions} $a$ \textbf{of} $\mathbb{R}^{d_z}$:
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\{\begin{aligned}
        E_r:x\in\mathcal{U}\mapsto&\mathbb{V}\left[\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)-\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)\right]\\
        e_r:x\in\mathcal{U}\mapsto&\underset{\|a\|_2=1}{\max}\left\|a^\top\left(\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)-\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)\right)\right\|_{L^2}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}\left(E_r(x)\right)}
        \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation*}\label{def:L2_error}
    \vspace{-\baselineskip}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    The interpolation error $e_f$ between \textbf{the full and subsampled estimators} is defined as:
    \begin{equation*}
        e_f:x\in\mathcal{U}\mapsto\left\|\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)-\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)\right\|_2
    \end{equation*}\label{def:L1_error}
    \vspace{-\baselineskip}
\end{definition}

\textbf{Both errors are well defined whatever the value of} $\beta$\textbf{: the two estimators being unbiased, their difference is a linear combination} $V^\top\tilde{\mathbf{z}}$ \textbf{with} $V^\top g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=0$\textbf{, whose distribution therefore does not depend on} $\beta$\textbf{. The subsampled estimator exists as long as} $g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ \textbf{has full column rank, which requires} $\tilde{N}^r\geq r_{\mathrm{UK}}$.

\textbf{[Manuscrit : renvoi à th:kernel\_SPSD.] Since} $\tilde{R}$ \textbf{may be singular,} $k$ \textbf{is assumed SPD, which makes} $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ \textbf{invertible, and} $\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ \textbf{with it, its Schur complement with respect to} $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ \textbf{being the negative definite matrix} $-M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{-1}$.

\section{Data Selection Errors}\label{sec:data_selection}

\subsection{Importance of the Gram Matrix}

As stated in \cref{sec:gaussian_process_regression}, most of the computational cost comes from inverting the Gram matrix. \textbf{Ordering the rows and columns of} $\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ \textbf{as} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$\textbf{, the} $r_{\mathrm{UK}}d_z$ \textbf{unbiasedness constraints, then} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d$, the decomposition introduced in \cref{sec:subsampling_errors} leads to the following block partitioning:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\B^\top&D\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)&\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)\\\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top&G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{with}\quad\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)\\g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
\textbf{the bordered vectors being reordered accordingly:}
\begin{equation}
    \bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,x)^\top&k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,x)^\top\end{bmatrix}^\top,\quad\bar{\mathbf{z}}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\bar{\mathbf{z}}^r\right)^\top&\left(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^d\right)^\top\end{bmatrix}^\top,\quad\bar{\mathbf{z}}^r\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r\right)^\top&0\end{bmatrix}^\top
\end{equation}

Let $\mathcal{G}_h(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ be the hollowed-out Gram matrix\textbf{, of the same size} $(\tilde{N}+r_{\mathrm{UK}})d_z$ \textbf{as} $\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G}_h(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)&0_{(\tilde{N}^r+r_{\mathrm{UK}})d_z,\tilde{N}^dd_z}\\0_{\tilde{N}^dd_z,(\tilde{N}^r+r_{\mathrm{UK}})d_z}&0_{\tilde{N}^dd_z,\tilde{N}^dd_z}\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\Cref{th:subsampled_kriging} states that $\mathcal{G}_h(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ acts as a substitute for $\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ in the subsampled case with respect to \eqref{eq:kriging_f} and \eqref{eq:kriging_R}.

\begin{proposition}
    The subsampled kriging estimator verifies:
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\{\begin{aligned}
        \hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)=&\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathcal{G}_h(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^\dagger\bar{\mathbf{z}}\\
        R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,g)=&k(x,x)+R-\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathcal{G}_h(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^\dagger\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)
        \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation*}
    where $\dagger$ denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse symbol \cite{bib:matrix-analysis}.\label{th:subsampled_kriging}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    The result comes from applying \eqref{eq:kriging_f} and \eqref{eq:kriging_R} to $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$, and noting that:
    \begin{equation*}
        \mathcal{G}_h(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^\dagger=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\vspace{-1.1\baselineskip}
    \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

Although it is not directly used in computations, \Cref{th:subsampled_kriging} further highlights the importance of the Gram matrix in the subsampling problem. In particular, \Cref{def:Gram_inverse_difference} serves as the foundation for the key reformulations of the studied errors established by \Cref{th:errors_inverse_Gram}.

\begin{definition}
    The Gram inverse difference matrix $\mathfrak{D}_G$ is the quantity defined as:
    \begin{equation*}
        \mathfrak{D}_G\triangleq\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{-1}-\mathcal{G}_h(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^\dagger
    \end{equation*}\label{def:Gram_inverse_difference}
    \vspace{-\baselineskip}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    The two subsampling errors can be respectively expressed as:
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\{\begin{aligned}
        E_r(x)=&\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathfrak{D}_G\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)\\
        e_f(x)=&\left\|\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathfrak{D}_G\bar{\mathbf{z}}\right\|_2
        \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation*}\label{th:errors_inverse_Gram}
    \vspace{-\baselineskip}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    See proof in \cref{sec:errors_inverse_Gram}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    An alternative reformulation of $E_r(x)$ is obtained in the demonstration of \Cref{th:errors_inverse_Gram}, and is worth comparing with the original definition of $e_f(x)$ given in \Cref{def:L1_error}.
    \begin{equation*}
        E_r(x)=R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,g)-R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},g)
    \end{equation*}
    In particular, $R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,g)\succeq R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},g)$\textbf{: subsampling can only inflate the covariance reported by the estimator, and by exactly the error it commits.}\label{rem:variance_difference}
\end{remark}

\subsection{Block Inversion and Residual Quantities}

To isolate the impact of discarding $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d$ on the kriging estimator, $\mathfrak{D}_G$ is decomposed using the Woodbury block matrix inversion formula \cite{bib:matrix-cookbook}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \mathfrak{D}_G=&\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\B^\top&D\end{bmatrix}^{-1}-\begin{bmatrix}A^{-1}&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\\
    =&\begin{bmatrix}A^{-1}BS_{d|r}^{-1}B^\top A^{-1}&-A^{-1}BS_{d|r}^{-1}\\-S_{d|r}^{-1}B^\top A^{-1}&S_{d|r}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\\
    =&\begin{bmatrix}-A^{-1}B\\I_{\tilde{N}^dd_z}\end{bmatrix}S_{d|r}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}-A^{-1}B\\I_{\tilde{N}^dd_z}\end{bmatrix}^\top
    \end{aligned}\label{eq:decomposed_gram_inverse_difference}
\end{equation}
where $S_{d|r}$ is the Schur complement of $\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ with respect to $A=\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$\textbf{, invertible since both} $\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ \textbf{and} $A$ \textbf{are}:
\begin{equation}
    S_{d|r}\triangleq D-B^\top A^{-1}B
\end{equation}

This decomposition, combined with \Cref{th:errors_inverse_Gram}, reveals two residual quantities that contribute to the error introduced by subsampling. The first one, called residual covariance function, quantifies the portion of the covariance between $x$ and the discarded training set $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d$ not accounted for by the retained set $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$. It is the object of \Cref{def:residual_covariance_function}. Similarly, the residual observation vector captures the information contained within the discarded observations $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^d$ and unexplained by the retained observations $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r$. Its expression is given by \Cref{def:residual_observation_vector}.

\begin{definition}
    The residual covariance function $k_{d|r}$ is given by:
    \begin{equation*}
        k_{d|r}:x\in\mathcal{U}\mapsto k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,x)-\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,x)
    \end{equation*}
    with $\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\triangleq\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top$.\label{def:residual_covariance_function}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    The residual observation vector $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}$ is defined as:
    \begin{equation*}
        \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}\triangleq\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^d-\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{z}}^r
    \end{equation*}\label{def:residual_observation_vector}
    \vspace{-\baselineskip}
\end{definition}

\Cref{th:residual_identities} gives relevant identities that link \eqref{eq:decomposed_gram_inverse_difference} with $k_{d|r}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}$. \Cref{th:errors_residual} and \Cref{th:errors_euclidean} push this further by showing that the studied metrics can be fully expressed as functions of those residual quantities\textbf{, which \Cref{th:explicit_residuals} relates to their simple kriging counterparts}.

\begin{lemma}
    The following residual identities hold:
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\{\begin{aligned}
        k_{d|r}(x)=&\begin{bmatrix}-A^{-1}B\\I_{\tilde{N}^dd_z}\end{bmatrix}^\top\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)\\
        k_{d|r}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)=&S_{d|r}-I_{\tilde{N}^d}\otimes\tilde{R}\\
        \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}=&\begin{bmatrix}-A^{-1}B\\I_{\tilde{N}^dd_z}\end{bmatrix}^\top\bar{\mathbf{z}}
        \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation*}\label{th:residual_identities}
    \vspace{-\baselineskip}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Recall that $A=\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ and $B=\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)$. Direct calculations give the desired results.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    The residual forms of the two errors are given below:
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\{\begin{aligned}
        E_r(x)=&k_{d|r}(x)^\top\left(k_{d|r}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)+I_{\tilde{N}^d}\otimes\tilde{R}\right)^{-1}k_{d|r}(x)\\
        e_f(x)=&\left\|k_{d|r}(x)^\top\left(k_{d|r}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)+I_{\tilde{N}^d}\otimes\tilde{R}\right)^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}\right\|_2
        \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation*}\label{th:errors_residual}
    \vspace{-\baselineskip}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    These expressions are obtained by applying \eqref{eq:decomposed_gram_inverse_difference} and \Cref{th:residual_identities} to \Cref{th:errors_inverse_Gram}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    \textbf{Let} $k^0_{d|r}$, $S^0_{d|r}$ \textbf{and} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^0_{d|r}$ \textbf{be the residual quantities of simple kriging, obtained by replacing the bordered Gram matrix with the Gram matrix:}
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\{\begin{aligned}
        k^0_{d|r}:x\in\mathcal{U}\mapsto&k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,x)-k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,x)\\
        S^0_{d|r}\triangleq&G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)-k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)\\
        \tilde{\mathbf{z}}^0_{d|r}\triangleq&\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^d-k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r
        \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation*}
    \textbf{and let} $U:x\in\mathcal{U}\mapsto g(x)-k(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ \textbf{be the weights given to the estimated trend coefficients by} $\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)$\textbf{, so that} $U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)=g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)-k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ \textbf{stacks its values on} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d$\textbf{. The residual quantities verify:}
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\{\begin{aligned}
        k_{d|r}(x)=&k^0_{d|r}(x)+U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)U(x)^\top\\
        S_{d|r}=&S^0_{d|r}+U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top\\
        \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}=&\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^0_{d|r}-U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)\hat{m}_{\mathrm{UK}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)
        \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation*}
    \textbf{In particular,} $S_{d|r}$ \textbf{is SPD, and} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}$ \textbf{is unchanged when a trend} $g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\gamma$, $\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{\mathrm{UK}}d_z}$\textbf{, is added to the observations.}\label{th:explicit_residuals}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    See proof in \cref{sec:explicit_residuals}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    \textbf{Let} $\|\cdot\|_{op}$ \textbf{denote the operator norm associated with the euclidean norm} $\|\cdot\|_2$\textbf{, and} let $\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle_{S_{d|r}}$ be the Mahalanobis inner product weighted by the inverse of $S_{d|r}=k_{d|r}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)+I_{\tilde{N}^d}\otimes\tilde{R}$\textbf{, which is SPD by \Cref{th:explicit_residuals}}:
    \begin{equation*}
        \forall n\in\mathbb{N}^*,\:\forall A,B\in\mathcal{M}_{\tilde{N}^dd_z,n}(\mathbb{R}),\:\langle A|B\rangle_{S_{d|r}}\triangleq A^\top S_{d|r}^{-1}B,\qquad\|A\|_{S_{d|r}}\triangleq\left\|S_{d|r}^{-\frac{1}{2}}A\right\|_{op}
    \end{equation*}
    The kriging errors can be interpreted as metrics in the euclidean space $(\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{N}^dd_z},\:\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle_{S_{d|r}})$\textbf{, to which the columns of} $k_{d|r}(x)$ \textbf{and} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}$ \textbf{belong}:
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\{\begin{aligned}
        E_r(x)=&\langle k_{d|r}(x)|k_{d|r}(x)\rangle_{S_{d|r}},\qquad e_r(x)=\|k_{d|r}(x)\|_{S_{d|r}}\\
        e_f(x)=&\left\|\langle k_{d|r}(x)|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}\rangle_{S_{d|r}}\right\|_2
        \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation*}\label{th:errors_euclidean}
    \vspace{-\baselineskip}
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
    \textbf{Contrary to simple kriging, discarding data uncorrelated with both} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$ \textbf{and} $x$ \textbf{is not free. If} $k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)=0$ \textbf{and} $k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,x)=0$\textbf{, then} $k^0_{d|r}(x)=0$, $U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)=g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)$ \textbf{and} $M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{-1}=M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}+g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^{-1}g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)$\textbf{. Applying the Woodbury formula \cite{bib:matrix-cookbook} to} $S_{d|r}=G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)+g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top$ \textbf{in \Cref{th:errors_euclidean} yields:}
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\{\begin{aligned}
        E_r(x)=&U(x)\left(M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)-M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right)U(x)^\top\\
        e_f(x)=&\left\|U(x)\left(\hat{m}_{\mathrm{UK}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)-\hat{m}_{\mathrm{UK}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)\right)\right\|_2
        \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation*}
    \textbf{The discarded data still reduce the covariance} $M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ \textbf{of the estimated trend coefficients, and both errors measure what is lost from that estimation, seen through the reliance} $U(x)$ \textbf{of the subsampled estimator on the trend. For ordinary kriging,} $E_r(x)$ \textbf{is the increase in covariance of the estimated mean, seen through} $U(x)$.\label{rem:mean_floor}
\end{remark}

\subsection{Relations Between the Errors}

This section introduces two relations between $e_r(x)$ and $e_f(x)$. The first relation is the concentration inequality described by \Cref{th:concentration_inequality}, while the second comes from algebraic considerations, and is given by \Cref{th:Cauchy-Schwarz_inequality}.

\begin{proposition}
    \textbf{Let} $\chi^2_{d_z}(1-\delta)$ \textbf{denote the quantile of order} $1-\delta$ \textbf{of the chi-squared distribution with} $d_z$ \textbf{degrees of freedom.} For $\delta\in(0,1]$:
    \begin{equation*}
        e_f(x)\leq\sqrt{\chi^2_{d_z}(1-\delta)}\cdot e_r(x)
    \end{equation*}
    with a probability of \textbf{at least} $1-\delta$.\label{th:concentration_inequality}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    \textbf{Whatever the value of} $\beta$, $\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)-\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)$ \textbf{follows a centred normal distribution of covariance} $E_r(x)$\textbf{, and can therefore be written} $E_r(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\xi$ \textbf{with} $\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d_z})$\textbf{. Thus:}
    \begin{equation*}
        e_f(x)^2=\xi^\top E_r(x)\xi\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(E_r(x)\right)\|\xi\|_2^2=e_r(x)^2\:\|\xi\|_2^2
    \end{equation*}
    \textbf{and} $\|\xi\|_2^2$ \textbf{follows a chi-squared distribution with} $d_z$ \textbf{degrees of freedom, so that} $\|\xi\|_2^2\leq\chi^2_{d_z}(1-\delta)$ \textbf{with a probability of} $1-\delta$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    \textbf{For a scalar field,} $\sqrt{\chi^2_1(1-\delta)}=\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)$\textbf{, where} $\Phi$ \textbf{is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution, and} a simpler, albeit more pessimistic, version of \Cref{th:concentration_inequality} can be derived from the inverse form of Mill's inequality \cite{bib:proba-course}:
    \begin{equation*}
        \forall\delta\in(0,1],\:\mathbb{P}\left(e_f(x)\leq\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\cdot e_r(x)\right)\geq1-\delta
    \end{equation*}
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}
    $e_r(x)$ and $e_f(x)$ are linked by the following inequality:
    \begin{equation*}
        e_f(x)\leq e_r(x)\cdot\|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}\|_{S_{d|r}}
    \end{equation*}\label{th:Cauchy-Schwarz_inequality}
    \vspace{-\baselineskip}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality \cite{bib:matrix-analysis} to \Cref{th:errors_euclidean} proves the relation.
\end{proof}

\section{Computable Error Bounds}\label{sec:error_bounds}

\textbf{The residual forms of \Cref{th:errors_euclidean} still involve} $S_{d|r}^{-1}$\textbf{, whose size} $\tilde{N}^dd_z$ \textbf{is that of the discarded observations, so that computing} $E_r(x)$ \textbf{exactly is as costly as the full kriging estimator that subsampling aims to avoid. This section derives upper bounds that do not require this inversion, first by separating the contributions of simple kriging and of the trend estimation, then by nesting the retained data into a larger set whose own subsampling error is small, and finally from entrywise bounds on the covariances between the data.}

\subsection{Simple Kriging and Trend Contributions}\label{sec:split_bound}

\textbf{\Cref{th:explicit_residuals} splits the residual covariance function into the residual} $k^0_{d|r}(x)$ \textbf{of simple kriging and a trend contribution. \Cref{th:split_bound} bounds these two parts separately, the latter being handled exactly as in \Cref{rem:mean_floor}.}

\begin{proposition}
    \textbf{Let} $e_{r,0}(x)\triangleq\|k^0_{d|r}(x)\|_{S^0_{d|r}}$ \textbf{be the error that simple kriging would commit if the trend were known, and} $P\triangleq U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top \left(S^0_{d|r}\right)^{-1}U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)$\textbf{. Then:}
    \begin{equation*}
        e_r(x)\leq e_{r,0}(x)+\sqrt{\left\|U(x)\left(M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)-\left(M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}+P\right)^{-1}\right)U(x)^\top\right\|_{op}}
    \end{equation*}
    \textbf{with equality when} $k^0_{d|r}(x)=0$.\label{th:split_bound}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    \textbf{See proof in \cref{sec:split_bound_proof}.}
\end{proof}

\textbf{The simple kriging term} $e_{r,0}(x)$ \textbf{still requires} $\left(S^0_{d|r}\right)^{-1}$\textbf{. When the observations are noisy, \Cref{th:computable_bound} removes this last inversion.}

\begin{corollary}
    \textbf{Assume that} $\tilde{R}\succ0$\textbf{, and let} $\tilde{\lambda}\triangleq\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{R})$\textbf{. Then} $S^0_{d|r}\succeq I_{\tilde{N}^d}\otimes\tilde{R}\succeq\tilde{\lambda}I_{\tilde{N}^dd_z}$\textbf{, and:}
    \begin{equation*}
        e_r(x)\leq e_r^U(x)\triangleq\frac{\|k^0_{d|r}(x)\|_{op}}{\sqrt{\tilde{\lambda}}}+\sqrt{\left\|U(x)\left(M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)-\left(M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}+\frac{U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)}{\tilde{\lambda}}\right)^{-1}\right)U(x)^\top\right\|_{op}}
    \end{equation*}\label{th:computable_bound}
    \vspace{-\baselineskip}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    \textbf{See proof in \cref{sec:split_bound_proof}.}
\end{proof}

\textbf{Unlike the residual forms,} $e_r^U(x)$ \textbf{only involves} $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}$\textbf{, the cross covariances} $k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ \textbf{and} $k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,x)$\textbf{, and the trend matrices, at a cost of} $\mathcal{O}\left(d_z^3\left(\left(\tilde{N}^r\right)^3+\tilde{N}^r\tilde{N}^d\right)\right)$ \textbf{that grows linearly with the number of discarded data. Through \Cref{th:concentration_inequality}, it also bounds the interpolation error,} $e_f(x)\leq\sqrt{\chi^2_{d_z}(1-\delta)}\cdot e_r^U(x)$ \textbf{holding with a probability of at least} $1-\delta$.

\begin{remark}
    \textbf{Along a fixed direction} $a\in\mathbb{R}^{d_z}$, $a^\top E_r(x)a$ \textbf{is the scalar quadratic form} $\left(k_{d|r}(x)a\right)^\top S_{d|r}^{-1}\left(k_{d|r}(x)a\right)$\textbf{. The cruder bound} $a^\top E_r(x)a\leq\|k_{d|r}(x)a\|_2^2/\tilde{\lambda}$\textbf{, which follows from} $S_{d|r}\succeq S^0_{d|r}\succeq I_{\tilde{N}^d}\otimes\tilde{R}$\textbf{, is its Gauss-Radau estimate with a single node prescribed at} $\tilde{\lambda}\leq\lambda_{\min}(S_{d|r})$ \textbf{\cite{bib:matrix-moments}. Running} $m$ \textbf{iterations of the Lanczos algorithm on} $S_{d|r}$ \textbf{from} $k_{d|r}(x)a$ \textbf{yields Gauss-Radau upper bounds that converge to} $a^\top E_r(x)a$ \textbf{as} $m$ \textbf{grows, at the cost of} $m$ \textbf{products by} $S_{d|r}$ \textbf{instead of its inversion.}\label{rem:gauss_radau}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    \textbf{The two terms of \Cref{th:split_bound} behave differently as the observation noise grows. When the noise is scaled up,} $\tilde{R}=\rho\tilde{R}_0$ \textbf{with} $\rho\rightarrow+\infty$ \textbf{and the other quantities fixed,} $e_{r,0}(x)$ \textbf{vanishes, discarded and retained data becoming equally uninformative about} $f(x)$\textbf{, whereas the trend term does not. Since} $U(x)\rightarrow g(x)$, $M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}\sim g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^\top\left(I_{\tilde{N}^r}\otimes\tilde{R}\right)^{-1}g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ \textbf{and} $P\sim g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top\left(I_{\tilde{N}^d}\otimes\tilde{R}\right)^{-1}g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)$\textbf{, it comes that, as soon as the right-hand side does not vanish:}
    \begin{equation*}
        E_r(x)\sim g(x)\left(\left(g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^\top\left(I_{\tilde{N}^r}\otimes\tilde{R}\right)^{-1}g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\right)^{-1}-\left(g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^\top\left(I_{\tilde{N}}\otimes\tilde{R}\right)^{-1}g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right)^{-1}\right)g(x)^\top
    \end{equation*}
    \textbf{For ordinary kriging, this equivalent is} $\left(1/\tilde{N}^r-1/\tilde{N}\right)\tilde{R}$\textbf{: both estimators become averages of the observations, and discarding data increases the covariance of the estimated mean.}\label{rem:noise_regime}
\end{remark}

\subsection{Nested Subsampling}\label{sec:nested_bound}

\textbf{The bound of \Cref{th:computable_bound} replaces} $\left(S^0_{d|r}\right)^{-1}$ \textbf{by} $\left(I_{\tilde{N}^d}\otimes\tilde{R}\right)^{-1}$\textbf{, which is pessimistic when} $k^0_{d|r}(x)$ \textbf{lies along the dominant eigenvectors of} $S^0_{d|r}$\textbf{. This happens when the discarded data are strongly correlated with the retained ones, typically just outside a spatial window, where the bound of \Cref{th:computable_bound} is at its loosest. \Cref{th:nested_bound} handles these data exactly, and only applies the bound to the remaining ones.}

\begin{proposition}
    \textbf{Let} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e$ \textbf{be a set of training data such that} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r\subset\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e\subset\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$\textbf{, and} $E_r^{(e)}(x)$ \textbf{be the error covariance committed by subsampling} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ \textbf{into} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e$\textbf{. Then, writing} $\Delta_e(x)\triangleq R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,g)-R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e,g)$:
    \begin{equation*}
        E_r(x)=\Delta_e(x)+E_r^{(e)}(x)
    \end{equation*}
    \textbf{Consequently, if} $\tilde{R}\succ0$ \textbf{and} $e_r^{U,(e)}(x)$ \textbf{denotes the bound of \Cref{th:computable_bound} for the retained set} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e$:
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\|\Delta_e(x)\right\|_{op}\leq e_r(x)^2\leq\left\|\Delta_e(x)\right\|_{op}+e_r^{U,(e)}(x)^2
    \end{equation*}\label{th:nested_bound}
    \vspace{-\baselineskip}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    \textbf{Applying \Cref{rem:variance_difference} to the subsamplings of} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ \textbf{into} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$ \textbf{and into} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e$ \textbf{gives} $E_r(x)=R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,g)-R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},g)$ \textbf{and} $E_r^{(e)}(x)=R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e,g)-R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},g)$\textbf{, whose difference is the announced identity. Both terms being SPSD,} $e_r(x)^2=\lambda_{\max}(E_r(x))$ \textbf{lies between} $\lambda_{\max}(\Delta_e(x))$ \textbf{and} $\lambda_{\max}(\Delta_e(x))+\lambda_{\max}\left(E_r^{(e)}(x)\right)$\textbf{, and \Cref{th:computable_bound} bounds the latter eigenvalue by} $e_r^{U,(e)}(x)^2$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{The identity states that the subsampling error accumulates along a nesting, each step adding the covariance it loses to the ones before it. Three properties follow. First,} $\Delta_e(x)$ \textbf{is computed exactly on the} $\tilde{N}^e$ \textbf{data of} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e$\textbf{, at the} $\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\tilde{N}^e\right)^3d_z^3\right)$ \textbf{cost of two kriging covariances, and is a lower bound on the error: it says how much of the loss is already certain, which no upper bound alone provides. Second, only the data outside} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e$ \textbf{are left to \Cref{th:computable_bound}, so the bracket tightens as} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e$ \textbf{grows, and its width is chosen by the user rather than dictated by the configuration. Third, when} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^e$ \textbf{gathers} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$ \textbf{and the discarded data lying within a few correlation lengths of} $x$\textbf{, the remaining data are, for most kernels used in practice, screened by the neighbouring ones \cite{bib:screening-effect}, so that} $e_r^{U,(e)}(x)$ \textbf{becomes small at a cost still far below that of the full estimator,} $\tilde{N}^e$ \textbf{remaining far below} $\tilde{N}$.

\subsection{Entrywise Bounds}\label{sec:entrywise_bounds}

\textbf{The previous bounds are evaluated on the data at hand. Bounds on the residual quantities can also be derived from bounds on the covariance blocks and on the observations, which make explicit how the errors depend on the correlations between the data. Since} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}$ \textbf{is unchanged when a trend is added to the observations, the latter are measured from an arbitrary reference trend} $g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\beta_0$, $\beta_0\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{\mathrm{UK}}d_z}$\textbf{, and the trend functions are assumed to verify} $\|g(x')\|_{op}\leq g_{\max}$ \textbf{for} $x'=x$ \textbf{and for every entry} $x'$ \textbf{of} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$\textbf{. Writing} $\tilde{x}^j_i$ \textbf{and} $\tilde{z}^j_i$ \textbf{for the} $i$\textbf{-th entries of} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^j$ \textbf{and} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^j$\textbf{, and} $\|K\|_{\max}$ \textbf{for the largest operator norm of the} $d_z\times d_z$ \textbf{blocks of a matrix} $K$\textbf{, which is its largest entry in absolute value when} $d_z=1$\textbf{, we assume the following blockwise bounds, for} $j\in\{r,d\}$ \textbf{and for all the indices involved:}
\begin{equation}
    \left\{\begin{aligned}
    \left\|k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\right\|_{\max}\leq&\alpha,\qquad\left\|k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,x)\right\|_{\max}\leq\kappa,\qquad\left\|k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,x)\right\|_{\max}\leq\eta\\
    \left\|k(\tilde{x}^j_i,\tilde{x}^j_l)\right\|_{op}\leq&\kappa\:\:\text{for}\:\:i\neq l,\qquad\left\|k(\tilde{x}^j_i,\tilde{x}^j_i)\right\|_{op}\leq k_{\max}\\
    \left\|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^j-g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^j)\beta_0\right\|_{\max}\leq&\tilde{z}_{\max}
    \end{aligned}\right.\label{eq:entrywise_assumptions}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Here,} $\kappa$ \textbf{bounds the covariances within} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$\textbf{, within} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d$\textbf{, and between} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$ \textbf{and} $x$\textbf{, while} $\alpha$ \textbf{and} $\eta$ \textbf{respectively bound the covariances between} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$ \textbf{and} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d$\textbf{, and between} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d$ \textbf{and} $x$\textbf{. The constants} $k_{\max}$ \textbf{and} $\tilde{z}_{\max}$ \textbf{respectively bound the prior covariances of the training data and the deviation of the observations from the reference trend, which can be minimised over} $\beta_0$\textbf{, the mid-range of} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}$ \textbf{being optimal for ordinary kriging on a scalar field. The subsampling strategy described in \cref{sec:subsampling_errors}, which retains the data close to} $x$ \textbf{and discards the farther ones, aims at the regime:}
\begin{equation}
    0<\eta,\alpha\ll\kappa\leq k_{\max}
\end{equation}

\textbf{We further define:}
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_j^-\triangleq\lambda_{\min}(G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^j)),\qquad\lambda_j^+\triangleq k_{\max}+(\tilde{N}^j-1)\kappa+\lambda_{\max}(\tilde{R}),\qquad j\in\{r,d\}
\end{equation}
\textbf{where} $\lambda_j^+$ \textbf{bounds} $\lambda_{\max}(G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^j))$ \textbf{by the blockwise Gershgorin circle theorem \cite{bib:matrix-analysis}, recalled in \cref{sec:entrywise_proof}. The conditioning of the trend on the retained data is measured by:}
\begin{equation}
    \tau_r\triangleq\frac{\tilde{N}^rg_{\max}^2}{\lambda_{\min}\left(g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^\top g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\right)}\geq r_{\mathrm{UK}}
\end{equation}
\textbf{which is equal to} $1$ \textbf{for ordinary kriging. \Cref{th:entrywise_residual} bounds the residual quantities, from which \Cref{th:entrywise_bounds} deduces bounds on the two errors.}

\begin{lemma}
    \textbf{Assume that \eqref{eq:entrywise_assumptions} holds and that} $\lambda_r^-\lambda_d^->\alpha^2\tilde{N}^r\tilde{N}^d$\textbf{. Then:}
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\{\begin{aligned}
        \|k_{d|r}(x)\|_{op}\leq k^{\max}_{d|r}\triangleq&\sqrt{\tilde{N}^d}\left(\frac{\lambda_r^-\eta+\alpha\tilde{N}^r\kappa}{\lambda_r^-}+\frac{\tau_r\lambda_r^+\rho_r(\lambda_r^-+\tilde{N}^r\kappa)}{\tilde{N}^r\lambda_r^-}\right)\\
        \|S_{d|r}^{-1}\|_{op}\leq s_{\max}\triangleq&\frac{\lambda_r^-}{\lambda_r^-\lambda_d^--\alpha^2\tilde{N}^r\tilde{N}^d}\\
        \|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}\|_2\leq\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^{\max}_{d|r}\triangleq&\sqrt{\tilde{N}^d}\rho_r\frac{\lambda_r^-+\tau_r\lambda_r^+}{\lambda_r^-}\tilde{z}_{\max}
        \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation*}
    \textbf{where} $\rho_r\triangleq(\lambda_r^-+\alpha\tilde{N}^r)/\lambda_r^-$.\label{th:entrywise_residual}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    \textbf{See proof in \cref{sec:entrywise_proof}.}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    \textbf{Under the assumptions of \Cref{th:entrywise_residual}, the two errors verify:}
    \begin{equation*}
        \left\{\begin{aligned}
        e_r(x)\leq&k^{\max}_{d|r}\sqrt{s_{\max}}\\
        e_f(x)\leq&k^{\max}_{d|r}\:\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^{\max}_{d|r}\:s_{\max}
        \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation*}
    \textbf{and} $\|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}\|_{S_{d|r}}\leq\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^{\max}_{d|r}\sqrt{s_{\max}}$.\label{th:entrywise_bounds}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    \textbf{For} $A$ \textbf{and} $B$ \textbf{with} $\tilde{N}^dd_z$ \textbf{rows,} $\left\|\langle A|B\rangle_{S_{d|r}}\right\|_{op}\leq\|A\|_{op}\cdot\|B\|_{op}\cdot\|S_{d|r}^{-1}\|_{op}$\textbf{. Applying this inequality and \Cref{th:entrywise_residual} to \Cref{th:errors_euclidean} concludes the proof.}
\end{proof}

\textbf{As in \Cref{th:Cauchy-Schwarz_inequality}, the bound on} $e_f(x)$ \textbf{is the product of the bounds on} $e_r(x)$ \textbf{and} $\|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}\|_{S_{d|r}}$\textbf{. Unlike their simple kriging counterparts, these bounds do not vanish when} $\alpha$ \textbf{and} $\eta$ \textbf{tend to} $0$\textbf{, their second term accounting for the estimation of the trend described by \Cref{rem:mean_floor}. They also decrease with} $\lambda_r^-$ \textbf{and} $\lambda_d^-$\textbf{, and thus remain valid when these eigenvalues are replaced by lower bounds such as} $\tilde{R}$\textbf{. Moreover, \cite{bib:smallest-eigenvalues} shows that the smallest eigenvalue of a covariance matrix remains bounded away from} $0$ \textbf{as the number of data grows, provided the training inputs are separated by a minimal distance.}

\begin{remark}
    \textbf{The condition} $\lambda_r^-\lambda_d^->\alpha^2\tilde{N}^r\tilde{N}^d$ \textbf{requires a gap between the retained and the discarded data, wide enough for their cross covariances to be small compared with the eigenvalues of the Gram matrices. When} $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$ \textbf{is a window cut out of a continuous set of training data,} $\alpha$ \textbf{is reached by the closest data on either side of its boundary, and is close to} $\kappa$\textbf{, so that \Cref{th:entrywise_bounds} does not apply and the bounds of \cref{sec:split_bound,sec:nested_bound} are to be used instead. Even when it applies, its successive triangular and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities make it loose, and its interest lies in the dependence it exhibits on} $\alpha$, $\eta$ \textbf{and} $\kappa$ \textbf{rather than in its numerical value.}\label{rem:entrywise_scope}
\end{remark}

\section{Conclusions}

\textbf{This paper derived explicit error bounds for universal kriging of a vector-valued field under training data subsampling, ordinary kriging being the special case of a constant mean. By decomposing the bordered Gramian covariance matrix of the observations and introducing residual quantities, we reformulated the subsampling errors into interpretable components, and related them to their simple kriging counterparts. This enabled the derivation of an upper bound separating the simple kriging error from the loss incurred on the estimation of the trend, computable without inverting any matrix of the size of the discarded data, as well as a nested decomposition that brackets the error between two computable quantities. Entrywise bounds on the kernel additionally express the errors in terms of the correlations within and between the retained and discarded data, although they only apply when both sets are well separated. Our analysis highlights how robustness to subsampling jointly depends on the correlation between the discarded and the retained training points, the observation noise, and the estimation of the unknown trend, to which even uncorrelated data contribute.}

Beyond clarifying the theoretical impact of subsampling, these results provide practical criteria for evaluating subsampling strategies, offering a principled alternative to heuristic selection rules. In particular, they make it possible to link the tolerated prediction error with the number of training points that can be discarded: if a maximum error level is specified, the bounds indicate how much data reduction is safe; conversely, given a subsampling strategy, they provide a quantifiable estimate of the induced error.

\subsection[]{Proving \Cref{th:errors_inverse_Gram}}\label{sec:errors_inverse_Gram}

\textbf{The weights of \eqref{eq:kriging_f} and the Lagrange multipliers} $\xi$ \textbf{of the unbiasedness constraints solve} $\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\begin{bmatrix}W\\\xi\end{bmatrix}=\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)$\textbf{, whose first block row reads} $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})W+g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\xi=k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)$\textbf{. The error} $e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\triangleq z-\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)$ \textbf{being centred whatever the value of} $\beta$\textbf{, and} $\varepsilon$ \textbf{being independent of} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}$\textbf{, it comes that:}
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\:e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}^\top\right]=k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)-G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})W=g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\xi
\end{equation*}
\textbf{so that} $e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}$ \textbf{is uncorrelated with every linear combination} $V^\top\tilde{\mathbf{z}}$ \textbf{such that} $V^\top g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=0$. The difference between the two interpolators can be decomposed as follows:
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)-\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)=\underbrace{\left(z-\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)\right)}_{e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r}\triangleq}-\underbrace{\left(z-\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)\right)}_{e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\triangleq}
\end{equation*}

Thus:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    E_r(x)=&\mathbb{E}\left[\left(e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r}-e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\right)\left(e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r}-e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\right)^\top\right]\\
    =&\mathbb{E}\left[e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r}e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r}^\top\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}^\top\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r}^\top\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r}e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}^\top\right]
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{The difference of the two interpolators being such a combination,} $\mathbb{E}\left[e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\left(e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r}-e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\right)^\top\right]=0$\textbf{, hence} $\mathbb{E}\left[e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r}^\top\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}^\top\right]$\textbf{, and so does its transpose. Moreover, both interpolators being unbiased, the second moments of their errors are their covariances. Therefore:}
\begin{equation*}
    E_r(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r}e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r}^\top\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}e_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}^\top\right]=R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,g)-R_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},g)
\end{equation*}

Using \Cref{th:subsampled_kriging} yields:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    E_r(x)=&k(x,x)+R-\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathcal{G}_h(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^\dagger\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)-k(x,x)-R+\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{-1}\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)\\
    =&\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathfrak{D}_G\bar{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},x)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

For $e_f(x)$, direct calculations give:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    e_f(x)=&\left\|\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}},g)-\hat{z}_{\mathrm{UK}}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)\right\|_2\\
    =&\left\|\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{z}}-\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathcal{G}_h(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^\dagger\bar{\mathbf{z}}\right\|_2\\
    =&\left\|\bar{k}(x,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\mathfrak{D}_G\bar{\mathbf{z}}\right\|_2
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

\subsection[]{Proving \Cref{th:explicit_residuals}}\label{sec:explicit_residuals}

\textbf{Writing} $G_r\triangleq G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$, $g_r\triangleq g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ \textbf{and} $M_r\triangleq M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ \textbf{for brevity, the block inversion of} $A=\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ \textbf{gives:}
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}G_r^{-1}-G_r^{-1}g_rM_rg_r^\top G_r^{-1}&G_r^{-1}g_rM_r\\M_rg_r^\top G_r^{-1}&-M_r\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Consequently, for} $x'\in\mathcal{U}$ \textbf{and for any matrices} $y$ \textbf{and} $t$ \textbf{with respectively} $\tilde{N}^rd_z$ \textbf{and} $r_{\mathrm{UK}}d_z$ \textbf{rows:}
\begin{equation*}
    \bar{k}(x',\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\mathcal{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}y\\t\end{bmatrix}=k(x',\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)G_r^{-1}y-U(x')M_r\left(t-g_r^\top G_r^{-1}y\right)
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Taking} $y=k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,x)$ \textbf{and} $t=g(x)^\top$ \textbf{for every} $x'\in\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d$ \textbf{gives the expression of} $k_{d|r}(x)$\textbf{, since} $t-g_r^\top G_r^{-1}y$ \textbf{is then equal to} $U(x)^\top$\textbf{. Taking the columns of} $k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)$ \textbf{and of} $g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top$ \textbf{for} $y$ \textbf{and} $t$ \textbf{gives the expression of} $S_{d|r}=D-B^\top A^{-1}B$ \textbf{in the same way, and} $y=\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r$ \textbf{with} $t=0$ \textbf{gives that of} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}$\textbf{, since} $g_r^\top G_r^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r=M_r^{-1}\hat{m}_{\mathrm{UK}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)$.

$S^0_{d|r}$ \textbf{is the Schur complement of the SPD matrix} $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ \textbf{with respect to} $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$\textbf{, and is therefore SPD \cite{bib:matrix-analysis}. Adding the SPSD matrix} $U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)M_rU(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top$ \textbf{keeps} $S_{d|r}$ \textbf{SPD. Finally, adding} $g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\gamma$ \textbf{to the observations adds} $U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)\gamma$ \textbf{to} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^0_{d|r}$ \textbf{and} $\gamma$ \textbf{to} $\hat{m}_{\mathrm{UK}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)$\textbf{, which leaves} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}$ \textbf{unchanged.}

\subsection[]{Proving \Cref{th:split_bound} and \Cref{th:computable_bound}}\label{sec:split_bound_proof}

\textbf{By \Cref{th:explicit_residuals},} $k_{d|r}(x)=k^0_{d|r}(x)+U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)U(x)^\top$ \textbf{and} $S_{d|r}=S^0_{d|r}+U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)^\top\succeq S^0_{d|r}$\textbf{, so that} $\|\cdot\|_{S_{d|r}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{S^0_{d|r}}$\textbf{. Applying the triangular inequality to the norm} $\|\cdot\|_{S_{d|r}}$ \textbf{in \Cref{th:errors_euclidean} thus gives:}
\begin{equation*}
    e_r(x)\leq\|k^0_{d|r}(x)\|_{S^0_{d|r}}+\left\|U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)U(x)^\top\right\|_{S_{d|r}}
\end{equation*}
\textbf{with equality when} $k^0_{d|r}(x)=0$\textbf{. Writing} $U_d\triangleq U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)$ \textbf{and} $M_r\triangleq M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ \textbf{for brevity, the Woodbury formula \cite{bib:matrix-cookbook} applied to} $S_{d|r}=S^0_{d|r}+U_dM_rU_d^\top$ \textbf{yields} $U_d^\top S_{d|r}^{-1}U_d=P-P(M_r^{-1}+P)^{-1}P=P(M_r^{-1}+P)^{-1}M_r^{-1}$\textbf{, hence:}
\begin{equation*}
    \left\|U_dM_rU(x)^\top\right\|_{S_{d|r}}^2=\left\|U(x)M_rP(M_r^{-1}+P)^{-1}U(x)^\top\right\|_{op}=\left\|U(x)\left(M_r-(M_r^{-1}+P)^{-1}\right)U(x)^\top\right\|_{op}
\end{equation*}
\textbf{which proves \Cref{th:split_bound}.}

\textbf{For \Cref{th:computable_bound}, note that} $S^0_{d|r}-I_{\tilde{N}^d}\otimes\tilde{R}$ \textbf{is the Schur complement of} $k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})+\begin{bmatrix}I_{\tilde{N}^r}&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\otimes\tilde{R}$ \textbf{with respect to} $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$\textbf{. This matrix being SPSD, so is its Schur complement \cite{bib:matrix-analysis}, and} $S^0_{d|r}\succeq I_{\tilde{N}^d}\otimes\tilde{R}\succeq\tilde{\lambda}I_{\tilde{N}^dd_z}$\textbf{. Consequently,} $\|k^0_{d|r}(x)\|_{S^0_{d|r}}\leq\|k^0_{d|r}(x)\|_{op}/\sqrt{\tilde{\lambda}}$ \textbf{and} $P\preceq U_d^\top U_d/\tilde{\lambda}$\textbf{. Since} $P\mapsto M_r-(M_r^{-1}+P)^{-1}$ \textbf{is non-decreasing for the Loewner order, applying these inequalities to \Cref{th:split_bound} concludes the proof.}

\subsection[]{Proving \Cref{th:entrywise_residual}}\label{sec:entrywise_proof}

\textbf{Let} $N(K)$ \textbf{gather the operator norms of the} $d_z\times d_z$ \textbf{blocks of a matrix} $K$\textbf{. Since} $\|Kv\|_2\leq\|N(K)u\|_2$\textbf{, where} $u$ \textbf{gathers the euclidean norms of the blocks of} $v$\textbf{, it comes that:}
\begin{equation*}
    \|K\|_{op}\leq\|N(K)\|_{op}\leq\sqrt{\|N(K)\|_1\:\|N(K)\|_\infty}
\end{equation*}
\textbf{the last two norms being the largest column and row sums of} $N(K)$ \textbf{\cite{bib:matrix-analysis}. Applied to the symmetric} $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^j)$\textbf{, this gives the blockwise Gershgorin bound} $\lambda_{\max}(G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^j))\leq\lambda_j^+$\textbf{, and applied to \eqref{eq:entrywise_assumptions}, it gives} $\|k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\|_{op}\leq\sqrt{\tilde{N}^r\tilde{N}^d}\alpha$, $\|k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,x)\|_{op}\leq\sqrt{\tilde{N}^r}\kappa$, $\|k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,x)\|_{op}\leq\sqrt{\tilde{N}^d}\eta$, $\|g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^j)\|_{op}\leq\sqrt{\tilde{N}^j}g_{\max}$ \textbf{and} $\|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^j-g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^j)\beta_0\|_2\leq\sqrt{\tilde{N}^j}\tilde{z}_{\max}$\textbf{. Since} $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)$ \textbf{is SPD, it follows that:}
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    \|k^0_{d|r}(x)\|_{op}\leq&\|k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,x)\|_{op}+\frac{\|k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\|_{op}\:\|k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,x)\|_{op}}{\lambda_r^-}\\
    \leq&\sqrt{\tilde{N}^d}\eta+\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{N}^r\tilde{N}^d}\alpha\cdot\sqrt{\tilde{N}^r}\kappa}{\lambda_r^-}=\sqrt{\tilde{N}^d}\frac{\lambda_r^-\eta+\alpha\tilde{N}^r\kappa}{\lambda_r^-}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{The same steps, applied to} $U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)$ \textbf{and} $U(x)$\textbf{, give:}
\begin{equation*}
    \|U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)\|_{op}\leq\sqrt{\tilde{N}^d}g_{\max}\rho_r,\qquad\|U(x)\|_{op}\leq g_{\max}\frac{\lambda_r^-+\tilde{N}^r\kappa}{\lambda_r^-}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Besides, for every unit vector} $v\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{\mathrm{UK}}d_z}$\textbf{, the Rayleigh quotient \cite{bib:matrix-analysis} and the definitions of} $\lambda_r^+$ \textbf{and} $\tau_r$ \textbf{give:}
\begin{equation*}
    v^\top M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}v=\left(g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)v\right)^\top G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}\left(g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)v\right)\geq\frac{\|g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)v\|_2^2}{\lambda_r^+}\geq\frac{\tilde{N}^rg_{\max}^2}{\tau_r\lambda_r^+}
\end{equation*}
\textbf{hence} $\|M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\|_{op}\leq\tau_r\lambda_r^+/(\tilde{N}^rg_{\max}^2)$\textbf{. Combining these inequalities with} $k_{d|r}(x)=k^0_{d|r}(x)+U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)U(x)^\top$ \textbf{from \Cref{th:explicit_residuals} through the triangular inequality gives the bound on} $\|k_{d|r}(x)\|_{op}$.

\textbf{Since} $S_{d|r}-S^0_{d|r}$ \textbf{is SPSD by \Cref{th:explicit_residuals},} $\lambda_{\min}(S_{d|r})\geq\lambda_{\min}(S^0_{d|r})$\textbf{. For every unit vector} $v\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{N}^dd_z}$:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    v^\top S^0_{d|r}v=&v^\top G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)v-\left(k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)v\right)^\top G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}\left(k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)v\right)\\
    \geq&\lambda_d^--\frac{\|k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)\|_{op}^2}{\lambda_r^-}\geq\frac{\lambda_r^-\lambda_d^--\alpha^2\tilde{N}^r\tilde{N}^d}{\lambda_r^-}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{This lower bound does not depend on} $v$\textbf{, and thus also bounds} $\lambda_{\min}(S^0_{d|r})$\textbf{, the minimum of} $v^\top S^0_{d|r}v$ \textbf{over the unit vectors. It is positive by assumption, so that} $\|S_{d|r}^{-1}\|_{op}=\lambda_{\min}(S_{d|r})^{-1}\leq s_{\max}$.

\textbf{Finally, since} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}$ \textbf{is unchanged when a trend is added to the observations, \Cref{th:explicit_residuals} can be applied to the observations} $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}-g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\beta_0$:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{d|r}=&\left(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^d-g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)\beta_0\right)-k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r-g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\beta_0\right)\\
    &-U(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^d)\left(\hat{m}_{\mathrm{UK}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)-\beta_0\right)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{The steps used for} $k^0_{d|r}(x)$ \textbf{bound the norm of the first two terms by} $\sqrt{\tilde{N}^d}\rho_r\tilde{z}_{\max}$\textbf{, while:}
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    \left\|\hat{m}_{\mathrm{UK}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r,g)-\beta_0\right\|_2=&\left\|M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^\top G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^r-g(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\beta_0\right)\right\|_2\\
    \leq&\frac{\tau_r\lambda_r^+}{\tilde{N}^rg_{\max}^2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{N}^r}g_{\max}\cdot\sqrt{\tilde{N}^r}\tilde{z}_{\max}}{\lambda_r^-}=\frac{\tau_r\lambda_r^+}{g_{\max}\lambda_r^-}\tilde{z}_{\max}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
\textbf{which concludes with the triangular inequality.}
