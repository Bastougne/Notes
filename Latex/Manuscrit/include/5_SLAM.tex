\section{Introduction}

Throughout \cref{sec:kriging_navigation}, we assumed that we had access to at least some prior knowledge of the environment in the form of a sparse and noisy set of training data. Such training points formed the basis on which we were able to reconstruct the observation function using kriging, without which no Bayesian correction could be performed.

In this chapter, we address the navigation problem described by \eqref{eq:navigation_problem} without any prior training data. This situation corresponds to simultaneous localisation and mapping (SLAM), where the environment must be learnt while simultaneously estimating the vehicle's position and kinematic state. SLAM is a well-established problem in autonomous navigation \cite{bib:SLAM-tutorial,bib:SLAM-survey}. A standard solution is to use exteroceptive sensors, such as a stereo camera \cite{bib:ORB-SLAM2}, LiDAR \cite{bib:LOAM,bib:Cartographer}, or sonar, which provide information about the surrounding environment beyond the vehicle's immediate vicinity.

In our case, however, we focus on local sensors, such as magnetometers or gravimeters, whose measurements are restricted to the field at the vehicle's current location. They consequently provide no direct information about the neighbouring environment, preventing the evaluation of the likelihood at any location other than the true one, which a Bayesian filter is precisely trying to recover.

GP-based maps have been used to overcome this limitation, by correlating the field at the current location with that of previously visited ones, both indoors \cite{bib:SLAM-magneto,bib:magnetic-SLAM-3D,bib:magnetic-SLAM-EKF} and from the air, over the magnetic anomaly field \cite{bib:MagSLAM} or the terrain \cite{bib:SLAM-GP-terrain}.

Similarly to \cref{sec:kriging_navigation}, additional assumptions on the environment must be made in order to make the problem tractable. In this chapter, we assume the field to be stationary, such that it can be fully represented by the same GP on the whole of $\mathbb{R}^{d_x}$ and across time. This is formally modelled by \Cref{th:SLAM_assumptions}.

\begin{assumption}[Stationary observation model]\label{th:SLAM_assumptions}
	The observation function is a time-invariant scalar GP, and its noise is an additive Gaussian white noise of variance $\mathbf{R}_k>0$:
	\begin{equation}
		z_k=h(x_k)+\varepsilon_k,\qquad h\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\kappa_\nu),\qquad \varepsilon_k\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{R}_k)
	\end{equation}
	where the mean of the GP is known, and thus assumed to be zero without loss of generality, and its covariance kernel is a Matérn kernel $\kappa_\nu$ with known hyperparameters, in the sense of \Cref{th:Matérn}.

	Unlike \Cref{th:kriging_navigation_assumptions}, no training data are available.
\end{assumption}

This chapter is organised as follows:
\begin{itemize}
	\item\Cref{sec:RBPF_SLAM} presents an existing SLAM method \cite{bib:magnetic-SLAM-3D} based on an RBPF formulation to estimate the coefficients of the Karhunen-Loève decomposition associated with the environment.
	\item\Cref{sec:RKHS_SLAM} describes a new method, which represents the field in an RKHS, and estimates its coefficients in this space within the AKKF.
	\item Finally, \cref{sec:SLAM_results} compares the two methods on a common simulation scenario.
\end{itemize}

\section{Rao-Blackwellised Mapping}\label{sec:RBPF_SLAM}

Without training data, the kriging estimators of \cref{sec:kriging} cannot be computed, and the observation function must be estimated alongside the state. However, a GP is an infinite-dimensional object, and cannot be added as such to the state of a filter.

The method proposed in \cite{bib:magnetic-SLAM-3D} addresses this issue by representing the field with a finite number of coefficients, which are then estimated jointly with the state by the RBPF of \cref{sec:particle_filtering}. This method is not a contribution of this thesis. It is presented in this section as the state-of-the-art approach to which the method proposed in \cref{sec:RKHS_SLAM} is compared.

In its original form, this method is applied to the three components of the magnetic field, modelled as the gradient of a scalar potential \cite{bib:magnetic-GP,bib:magnetic-interpolation}, and the domain is divided into hexagonal tiles, each associated with its own set of coefficients. For the sake of simplicity, it is presented here for a scalar field and a single domain.

\subsection{Reduced-Rank Field Representation}

According to \Cref{th:KL}, a GP defined on a compact set can be decomposed over the eigenfunctions of its Mercer operator. Truncating this decomposition at rank $r_{\mathrm{KL}}\in\mathbb{N}^*$ gives the finite-dimensional parametrisation of \Cref{th:KL_parametrisation}, in which the field is described by $r_{\mathrm{KL}}$ coefficients. Since the observed field only depends on the position of the vehicle, its representation is defined on the position subspace, of dimension $d_{\mathrm{pos}}$. When a state $x$ is given as argument of $h$ or of the basis functions, it thus stands for its position $\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\mathrm{pos}}\:x$. Formally, we assume that the position of the vehicle remains within a bounded domain:
\begin{equation}
	\Omega\triangleq\prod_{d=1}^{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}[a_d,b_d]
\end{equation}
where $a_1,\cdots,a_{d_{\mathrm{pos}}},b_1,\cdots,b_{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}\in\mathbb{R}$, and we let $L_d\triangleq b_d-a_d$ denote its length along the $d$-th axis.

As noted in \Cref{th:eigenpairs}, the eigenpairs of the Mercer operator are not available in closed form in general. For a radial kernel, they can be approximated using the eigenfunctions of the Laplace operator on $\Omega$ with Dirichlet boundary conditions, which are known explicitly \cite{bib:reduced-rank}. Let $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_{r_{\mathrm{KL}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}$ denote the $r_{\mathrm{KL}}$ vectors of smallest norm among the frequencies:
\begin{equation}
	\pi\begin{bmatrix}
	\dfrac{n_1}{L_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{n_{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}}{L_{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}}
	\end{bmatrix}^\top,\qquad n_1,\dots,n_{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}\in\mathbb{N}^*
\end{equation}

The eigenpairs of the Mercer operator are then approximated by:
\begin{equation}
	\left\{\begin{aligned}
	\psi_j&:x\in\Omega\mapsto\prod_{d=1}^{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{L_d}}\:\sin\left(\omega_j^{(d)}\left(x^{(d)}-a_d\right)\right)\\
	\lambda_j&=S_\nu(\omega_j)
	\end{aligned}\right.\label{eq:Laplace_basis}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_j^{(d)}$ and $x^{(d)}$ denote the $d$-th components of $\omega_j$ and $x$, and $S_\nu$ is the Fourier transform of $\kappa_\nu$ given in \cref{sec:proof_Matérn}. $S_\nu$ is  also known as the spectral density of $\kappa_\nu$.

Let $\boldsymbol{\Psi}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$ be defined as in \cref{sec:efficient_kriging} from these approximated eigenpairs. Under \Cref{th:SLAM_assumptions}, \Cref{th:KL_parametrisation} then gives:
\begin{equation}
	\forall x\in\Omega,\:h(x)\approx\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x)\:\boldsymbol{\gamma},\qquad\boldsymbol{\gamma}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\label{eq:SLAM_map}
\end{equation}

Since $S_\nu$ is only given up to the constant $C$ in \cref{sec:proof_Matérn}, the eigenvalues are normalised such that the variance $\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x)\:\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x)^\top$ of the approximated field equals $\sigma_h^2$ at the centre of $\Omega$, where the boundary conditions have the least influence. Furthermore, the basis functions only depend on the domain, and can thus be computed before the mission.

\begin{remark}[Choice of the domain]
	The basis functions of \eqref{eq:Laplace_basis} are equal to zero on the boundary of $\Omega$, and so is the field reconstructed from \eqref{eq:SLAM_map}. The domain must therefore be chosen larger than the region covered by the vehicle. However, the frequencies are spaced by $\pi/L_d$ along the $d$-th axis. The rank $r_{\mathrm{KL}}$ required to reach a given frequency thus increases with the size of the domain, which is why the original method divides it into tiles.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Rao-Blackwellised Estimation}

Under \Cref{th:SLAM_assumptions} and the approximation \eqref{eq:SLAM_map}, the observation equation becomes:
\begin{equation}
	z_k=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x_k)\:\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\varepsilon_k
\end{equation}
and is thus linear in the coefficients for a given state. Assuming that the evolution noise is additive and Gaussian, the navigation problem \eqref{eq:navigation_problem} is then the system \eqref{eq:RBPF_model} with:
\begin{equation}
	\left\{\begin{array}{llll}
	x_k^n=x_k&\mathbf{F}_k^n=0&f_k^n=f_k&\mathbf{Q}_k^n=\mathbf{Q}_k\\
	x_k^l=\boldsymbol{\gamma}&\mathbf{F}_k^l=\mathbf{I}_{r_{\mathrm{KL}}}&f_k^l=0&\mathbf{Q}_k^l=0\\
	\mathbf{H}_k=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\circ\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\mathrm{pos}}&h_k=0&\mathbf{Q}_k^{nl}=0&
	\end{array}\right.
\end{equation}

Since $\mathbf{F}_k^n=0$, the propagation of the state does not depend on the coefficients, and the Kalman pre-correction \eqref{eq:RBPF_precorrection_debut} through \eqref{eq:RBPF_precorrection_fin} is omitted. Moreover, since the field does not vary over time, the coefficients are constant, and the Kalman prediction \eqref{eq:RBPF_prediction_debut} through \eqref{eq:RBPF_prediction_fin} leaves their estimate and its covariance unchanged. For the sake of clarity, we thus write $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k^{(i)}$ and $\mathbf{P}_k^{\gamma(i)}$ for the estimate $\hat{x}_{k|k}^{l(i)}$ of the coefficients associated with the $i$-th particle and for its covariance $\mathbf{P}_{k|k}^{l(i)}$. They are initialised with the prior distribution of \eqref{eq:SLAM_map}:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0^{(i)}=0\quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf{P}_0^{\gamma(i)}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
\end{equation}

Using the transition density as proposal, as described in \eqref{eq:prior_proposal}, each iteration of the RBPF is then computed in three steps:
\begin{itemize}
	\item\textbf{Particle propagation.} The particles are propagated according to the transition density:
	\begin{equation}
		x_k^{(i)}\sim p\left(\cdot|x_{k-1}^{(i)}\right)
	\end{equation}
	\item\textbf{Weight update.} Let $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\triangleq\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(x_k^{(i)}\right)$. The innovation and its covariance are:
	\begin{equation}
		\left\{\begin{aligned}
		y_k^{(i)}&=z_k-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\:\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}^{(i)}\\
		\mathbf{S}_k^{(i)}&=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\:\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^{\gamma(i)}\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\right)^\top+\mathbf{R}_k+\max\left(\sigma_h^2-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\:\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\right)^\top,0\right)
		\end{aligned}\right.\label{eq:SLAM_innovation}
	\end{equation}
	and the weights are updated as in \eqref{eq:weight_update_prior}, with the likelihood:
	\begin{equation}
		z_k|\mathbf{x}_{0:k}^{(i)},\mathbf{z}_{1:k-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\:\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}^{(i)},\mathbf{S}_k^{(i)}\right)
	\end{equation}

	Similarly to \eqref{eq:kriged_likelihood}, its covariance accounts for both the uncertainty of the estimated field and the observation noise. The last term of $\mathbf{S}_k^{(i)}$, which is not part of the original method, is the part of the prior variance $\sigma_h^2$ that the truncated basis does not represent. Since it cannot be learnt, it is treated as an additional observation noise.
	\item\textbf{Kalman correction.} The coefficients associated with each particle are corrected as:
	\begin{equation}
		\left\{\begin{aligned}
		\mathbf{K}_k^{\gamma(i)}&=\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^{\gamma(i)}\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\right)^\top\left(\mathbf{S}_k^{(i)}\right)^{-1}\\
		\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k^{(i)}&=\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}^{(i)}+\mathbf{K}_k^{\gamma(i)}\:y_k^{(i)}\\
		\mathbf{P}_k^{\gamma(i)}&=\left(\mathbf{I}_{r_{\mathrm{KL}}}-\mathbf{K}_k^{\gamma(i)}\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\right)\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^{\gamma(i)}
		\end{aligned}\right.
	\end{equation}
\end{itemize}

The state and the coefficients are then estimated as in \cref{sec:particle_filtering}, by the weighted means:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{x}_k=\sum_{i=1}^Nw_k^{(i)}\:x_k^{(i)}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k=\sum_{i=1}^Nw_k^{(i)}\:\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k^{(i)}
\end{equation}
and the field is reconstructed as $x\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x)\:\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k$.

As for the RPF of \Cref{alg:RPF}, the particles are only resampled when the effective sample size falls below $N_{\mathrm{th}}$, and then regularised. In this case, the coefficients and their covariance are duplicated along with their particle, and the innovation is computed again at the regularised particles before the Kalman correction. It must be noted that the regularisation is not part of the original method. Since it only moves the states, each set of coefficients, which is conditioned on the trajectory of its parent, is corrected at a perturbed position, which is an approximation. The resulting algorithm, referred to as RBPF-SLAM, is given by \Cref{alg:SLAM_RBPF}, where $i^{(j)}$ denotes the parent index of the $j$-th resampled particle.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
	\begin{algorithmic}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:x_k^{(i)}\sim p\left(\cdot|x_{k-1}^{(i)}\right)$}\Comment{Particle propagation}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(x_k^{(i)}\right)$}\Comment{\textbf{Innovation}}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:y_k^{(i)}=z_k-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\:\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}^{(i)}$}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:\mathbf{S}_k^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\:\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^{\gamma(i)}\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\right)^\top+\mathbf{R}_k+\max\left(\sigma_h^2-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\:\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\right)^\top,0\right)$}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:z_k|\mathbf{x}_{0:k}^{(i)},\mathbf{z}_{1:k-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\:\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}^{(i)},\mathbf{S}_k^{(i)}\right)$}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:\tilde{w}_k^{(i)}=w_{k-1}^{(i)}\:\tilde{p}\left(z_k|x_k^{(i)}\right)$}\Comment{Weight update}
	\State{$\displaystyle\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:w_k^{(i)}=\left.\tilde{w}_k^{(i)}\middle/\sum_{j=1}^N\tilde{w}_k^{(j)}\right.$}\Comment{Normalisation}
	\State{$\displaystyle\hat{N}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\left.1\middle/\sum_{i=1}^N\left(w_k^{(i)}\right)^2\right.$}\Comment{Resampling criterion}
	\If{$\hat{N}_{\mathrm{eff}}<N_{\mathrm{th}}$}
	\State{$\displaystyle\hat{x}_k=\sum_{i=1}^Nw_k^{(i)}x_k^{(i)}$}\Comment{Whitening}
	\State{$\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{P}}_k=\sum_{i=1}^Nw_k^{(i)}\left(x_k^{(i)}-\hat{x}_k\right)\left(x_k^{(i)}-\hat{x}_k\right)^\top$}
	\State{$\mathbf{A}_k\text{ s.t. }\hat{\mathbf{P}}_k=\mathbf{A}_k\:\mathbf{A}_k^\top$}
	\State{$\left\{x_k^{(j)},w_k^{(j)},i^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^N\gets\operatorname{Resampling}\left(\left\{x_k^{(i)},w_k^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^N\right)$}\Comment{Resampling}
	\State{$\forall j\in[\![1,N]\!],\:\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}^{(j)},\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^{\gamma(j)}\right)\gets\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}^{\left(i^{(j)}\right)},\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^{\gamma\left(i^{(j)}\right)}\right)$}\Comment{\textbf{Map duplication}}
	\State{$\forall j\in[\![1,N]\!],\:\varepsilon^{(j)}\sim K$}\Comment{Regularisation}
	\State{$\forall j\in[\![1,N]\!],\:x_k^{(j)}\gets x_k^{(j)}+\alpha_{\mathrm{RPF}}\ell \mathbf{A}_k\:\varepsilon^{(j)}$}
	\State{$\forall j\in[\![1,N]\!],\:$recompute $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(j)}$, $y_k^{(j)}$ and $\mathbf{S}_k^{(j)}$}\Comment{\textbf{Innovation}}
	\EndIf
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:\mathbf{K}_k^{\gamma(i)}=\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^{\gamma(i)}\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\right)^\top\left(\mathbf{S}_k^{(i)}\right)^{-1}$}\Comment{\textbf{Kalman correction}}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k^{(i)}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}^{(i)}+\mathbf{K}_k^{\gamma(i)}\:y_k^{(i)}$}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:\mathbf{P}_k^{\gamma(i)}=\left(\mathbf{I}_{r_{\mathrm{KL}}}-\mathbf{K}_k^{\gamma(i)}\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^{(i)}\right)\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^{\gamma(i)}$}
	\end{algorithmic}\caption{Rao-Blackwellised SLAM}\label{alg:SLAM_RBPF}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Cost of RBPF-SLAM]\label{th:SLAM_cost}
	Each particle is associated with its own coefficients and covariance matrix. Each iteration of \Cref{alg:SLAM_RBPF} thus requires $\mathcal{O}\left(N\:r_{\mathrm{KL}}^2\right)$ operations, and the storage of $\mathcal{O}\left(N\:r_{\mathrm{KL}}^2\right)$ values. Unlike kriging, these costs do not depend on the number of observations collected since the beginning of the mission.
\end{remark}

\section{RKHS-Based Mapping}\label{sec:RKHS_SLAM}

As described by \Cref{th:SLAM_cost}, RBPF-SLAM associates each particle with its own coefficients and covariance matrix, which becomes costly when the number of particles or of basis functions is large. In this section, we propose to estimate a single set of coefficients instead, shared by all the particles. The proposed method relies on the AKKF of \Cref{alg:AKKF}.

The AKKF never evaluates the likelihood, and only requires samples of $p(z_k|x_k)$. However, under \Cref{th:SLAM_assumptions}, drawing these samples requires the observation function $h$, which is unknown. Since the filtering problem is already formulated in an RKHS, it is natural to estimate $h$ in such a space as well. This section introduces random Fourier features (RFF) \cite{bib:RFF,bib:RFF-survey} to obtain a finite-dimensional approximation of this space, and recursive least squares (RLS) \cite{bib:RFF-RLS} to estimate the corresponding coefficients.

\subsection{Random Fourier Features}

Let $\mathcal{H}_{\kappa_\nu}$ be the native RKHS of the kernel $\kappa_\nu$. According to \Cref{th:RKHS_regularisation}, the regularised least-squares estimate of $h$ in $\mathcal{H}_{\kappa_\nu}$ is a linear combination of the features of its training points. As for kriging, its number of coefficients is thus equal to the number of training points, which increases at each time step when the observations are collected along the trajectory. To obtain a fixed number of coefficients, the kernel is approximated by a finite number of known functions instead.

Let $p_\nu$ be the probability density obtained by normalising the spectral density of $\kappa_\nu$:
\begin{equation}
	p_\nu:\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}\mapsto\left.S_\nu(\omega)\middle/\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}}{S_\nu(\omega')\:d\omega'}\right.
\end{equation}

In particular, $p_\nu$ is the density of a centred multivariate Student distribution with $2\nu$ degrees of freedom and scale matrix $\ell^{-2}\mathbf{I}_{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}$, which becomes Gaussian when $\nu=+\infty$.

According to Bochner's theorem, already used in \cref{sec:proof_Matérn}, a continuous stationary kernel is SPSD if and only if it is the Fourier transform of a finite positive measure. For $\kappa_\nu$, the density of this measure is proportional to $S_\nu$, and thus to $p_\nu$. Since $\kappa_\nu$ is real-valued and $\kappa_\nu(x,x)=\sigma_h^2$, this gives:
\begin{equation}
	\forall x,x'\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{pos}}},\:\kappa_\nu(x,x')=\sigma_h^2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}}{\cos\left(\omega^\top(x-x')\right)p_\nu(\omega)\:d\omega}
\end{equation}

Let $\omega$ be a random variable of probability density $p_\nu$, and $b\sim\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi])$ be independent of $\omega$. We have:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
	\mathbb{E}\left[2\cos\left(\omega^\top x+b\right)\cos\left(\omega^\top x'+b\right)\right]&=\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\omega^\top(x-x')\right)\right]\\
	&\qquad+\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\omega^\top(x+x')+2b\right)\right]\\
	&=\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\omega^\top(x-x')\right)\right]
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
since the expectation of the second term with respect to $b$ is zero. The kernel can thus be written as an expectation:
\begin{equation}
	\kappa_\nu(x,x')=\sigma_h^2\:\mathbb{E}\left[2\cos\left(\omega^\top x+b\right)\cos\left(\omega^\top x'+b\right)\right]
\end{equation}

Similarly to \Cref{th:MC_estimator}, this expectation can be approximated by a Monte Carlo estimator. Let $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}\in\mathbb{N}^*$, and let $(\omega_j,b_j)_{j\in[\![1,r_{\mathrm{RFF}}]\!]}$ be i.i.d. draws of $(\omega,b)$. In this section, $\psi_j$ and $\boldsymbol{\Psi}$ denote the corresponding features:
\begin{equation}
	\psi_j:x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}\mapsto\sqrt{\frac{2}{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}}\:\cos\left(\omega_j^\top x+b_j\right)
\end{equation}
and:
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x)\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}
	\psi_1(x)&\cdots&\psi_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}(x)
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

The kernel is then approximated by:
\begin{equation}
	\kappa_\nu(x,x')\approx\sigma_h^2\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x)\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x')^\top
\end{equation}

Every function of the native RKHS of this approximated kernel is a linear combination of $\psi_1,\dots,\psi_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}$. The observation function is thus estimated as:
\begin{equation}
	\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{pos}}},\:h(x)\approx\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x)\:\boldsymbol{\gamma}\label{eq:RFF_map}
\end{equation}
with $\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}$. This representation has the same form as \eqref{eq:SLAM_map}. However, its basis functions are defined on the whole of $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}$, and do not depend on any domain.

\subsection{Recursive Least Squares}

The coefficients of \eqref{eq:RFF_map} are estimated from the observations collected by the vehicle. Since the true states are unknown, each observation $z_l$ is associated with the state estimate $\hat{x}_l$ of the filter at time $l$. Formally, the estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k$ of the coefficients at time $k$ is defined as the minimiser of the regularised least-squares criterion:
\begin{equation}
	J_k:\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}\mapsto\sum_{l=1}^k\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}^{k-l}\left(z_l-\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_l\right)\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)^2+\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}^k\alpha_{\mathrm{RLS}}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_2^2\label{eq:RLS_criterion}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{\mathrm{RLS}}\in\mathbb{R}_+^*$ is a regularisation coefficient, and $\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}\in(0,1]$ is a forgetting factor, which reduces the influence of the oldest observations when $\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}<1$.

Minimising \eqref{eq:RLS_criterion} at each time step would require storing every pair $\left(\hat{x}_l,z_l\right)$. \Cref{th:RLS} shows that $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k$ can instead be computed from $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}$ and the last pair alone, which is known as the RLS algorithm.

\begin{proposition}[Recursive least squares]\label{th:RLS}
	Let $k\in\mathbb{N}^*$. The criterion \eqref{eq:RLS_criterion} admits a unique minimiser $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k$, which verifies:
	\begin{equation}
		\left\{\begin{aligned}
		\mathbf{K}_k^\gamma&=\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_k\right)^\top\left(\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_k\right)\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_k\right)^\top\right)^{-1}\\
		\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k&=\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}+\mathbf{K}_k^\gamma\left(z_k-\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_k\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}\right)\\
		\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma&=\frac{1}{\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}-\mathbf{K}_k^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_k\right)\right)\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma
		\end{aligned}\right.\label{eq:RLS}
	\end{equation}
	with:
	\begin{equation}
		\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0=0\quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf{P}_0^\gamma=\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mathrm{RLS}}}\mathbf{I}_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}
	\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
	Writing $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_l$ for $\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_l\right)$, we let:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma\triangleq\left(\sum_{l=1}^k\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}^{k-l}\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_l^\top\boldsymbol{\Psi}_l+\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}^k\alpha_{\mathrm{RLS}}\mathbf{I}_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}\right)^{-1}
	\end{equation}
	which is well defined as the inverse of the sum of an SPSD and an SPD matrix. In particular, $\mathbf{P}_0^\gamma$ is the matrix given above. Since the Hessian of $J_k$ is $2\left(\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma\right)^{-1}\succ0$, this function is strictly convex. It is therefore minimised where its derivative vanishes:
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{aligned}
		&\frac{\partial J_k}{\partial\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=0\\
		\iff\quad&\sum_{l=1}^k\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}^{k-l}\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_l^\top\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_l\:\boldsymbol{\gamma}-z_l\right)+\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}^k\alpha_{\mathrm{RLS}}\:\boldsymbol{\gamma}=0\\
		\iff\quad&\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma\sum_{l=1}^k\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}^{k-l}\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_l^\top z_l
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
	which gives the unique minimiser $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k$. This expression also holds for $k=0$ with $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0=0$.

	By definition, we have:
	\begin{equation}
		\left\{\begin{aligned}
		\left(\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma\right)^{-1}&=\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\right)^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^\top\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k\\
		\left(\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma\right)^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k&=\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\right)^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^\top z_k
		\end{aligned}\right.
	\end{equation}

	The Woodbury inversion formula (see \Cref{th:Woodbury}) applied to the first equation gives:
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{aligned}
		\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma&=\frac{1}{\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma-\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^\top\left(\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k\:\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^\top\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k\:\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\right)\\
		&=\frac{1}{\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}-\mathbf{K}_k^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k\right)\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
	and thus:
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{aligned}
		\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^\top&=\frac{1}{\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}}\left(\mathbf{K}_k^\gamma\left(\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k\:\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^\top\right)-\mathbf{K}_k^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k\:\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^\top\right)\\
		&=\mathbf{K}_k^\gamma
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}

	Combining these results finally yields:
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{aligned}
		\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k&=\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma\left(\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\right)^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^\top z_k\right)\\
		&=\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma\left(\left(\left(\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma\right)^{-1}-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^\top\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k^\top z_k\right)\\
		&=\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}+\mathbf{K}_k^\gamma\left(z_k-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k\:\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}\right)
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Integration into the AKKF}

Since \eqref{eq:RLS} only involves the pair $\left(\hat{x}_k,z_k\right)$, the coefficients can be updated at each time step, within the recursion of the AKKF. Each iteration of the resulting filter is then computed in four steps:
\begin{itemize}
	\item\textbf{Embedded prediction.} The particles are propagated through the evolution model, and the weights and their covariance are predicted as in \Cref{alg:AKKF}.
	\item\textbf{Embedded correction.} Since $h$ is unknown, the simulated observations are generated from the current estimate of the field:
	\begin{equation}
		\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:z_k^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(x_k^{(i)}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}+\varepsilon_k^{(i)}
	\end{equation}
	where $\varepsilon_k^{(i)}$ is drawn from a centred normal distribution of variance:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{R}_k\left(1+\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(x_k^{(i)}\right)\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(x_k^{(i)}\right)^\top\right)\label{eq:AKKF_SLAM_variance}
	\end{equation}

	Similarly to the innovation covariance $\mathbf{S}_k^{(i)}$ of \eqref{eq:SLAM_innovation}, this variance accounts for both the observation noise and the uncertainty of the estimated field. Indeed, when $\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}=1$ and $\mathbf{R}_k$ does not depend on $k$, \eqref{eq:RLS} gives the posterior mean $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}$ and covariance $\mathbf{R}_k\:\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma$ of the coefficients given the first $k-1$ observations, under the prior distribution $\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathbf{R}_k\:\mathbf{P}_0^\gamma\right)$. The variance of $z_k$ given these observations and $x_k=x_k^{(i)}$ is then that of $\varepsilon_k^{(i)}$.

	The correction of the weights is then that of \Cref{alg:AKKF}, whereas the state estimate and its covariance are computed with respect to the empirical mean $\bar{x}_k$ of the particles.
	\item\textbf{Map update.} The coefficients $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k$ are computed with \eqref{eq:RLS}, from the real observation $z_k$ and the state estimate $\hat{x}_k$.
	\item\textbf{Resampling.} The new particles are drawn from $\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{x}_k,\hat{\mathbf{P}}_k\right)$, and the embedded resampling is performed as in \Cref{alg:AKKF}.
\end{itemize}

It must be noted that the map update only relies on the real observation $z_k$, whereas the correction step uses the observations simulated from the estimated field. The resulting algorithm, referred to as AKKF-SLAM and originally proposed in \cite{bib:RFF-KKF-SLAM}, is given by \Cref{alg:AKKF_SLAM}. In its original version, however, the simulated observations only include the observation noise, and the moments are computed as in \Cref{alg:AKKF}.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
	\begin{algorithmic}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:x_k^{(i)}\sim p\left(\cdot|x_{k-1}^{+(i)}\right)$}\Comment{Particle propagation}
	\State{$\mathbf{D}_k=\left(\mathbf{G}_{k-1}^x+\alpha_{\mathrm{RKHS},x}\mathbf{I}_N\right)^{-1}\mathbf{G}_{k-1}^x-\mathbf{I}_N$}\Comment{Embedded prediction}
	\State{$\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k|k-1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k-1|k-1}^++\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{D}_k\:\mathbf{D}_k^\top$}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:z_k^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(x_k^{(i)}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}+\varepsilon_k^{(i)},$}\Comment{\textbf{Simulated observations}}
	\State{$\qquad\varepsilon_k^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathbf{R}_k\left(1+\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(x_k^{(i)}\right)\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(x_k^{(i)}\right)^\top\right)\right)$}
	\State{$\mathbf{K}_k=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k|k-1}\left(\mathbf{G}_k^z\:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k|k-1}+\alpha_{\mathrm{RKHS},z}\mathbf{I}_N\right)^{-1}$}\Comment{Embedded correction}
	\State{$\mathbf{w}_k=\mathbf{w}_{k-1}^++\mathbf{K}_k\left(\kappa_Z\left(\mathbf{z}_k,z_k\right)-\mathbf{G}_k^z\:\mathbf{w}_{k-1}^+\right)$}
	\State{$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k|k}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k|k-1}-\mathbf{K}_k\:\mathbf{G}_k^z\:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k|k-1}$}
	\State{$\displaystyle\bar{x}_k=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^Nx_k^{(i)}$}\Comment{\textbf{State estimation}}
	\State{$\displaystyle\hat{x}_k=\bar{x}_k+\sum_{i=1}^Nw_k^{(i)}\left(x_k^{(i)}-\bar{x}_k\right)$}
	\State{$\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{P}}_k=\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^N\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k|k}\right]_{ij}\left(x_k^{(i)}-\bar{x}_k\right)\left(x_k^{(j)}-\bar{x}_k\right)^\top$}
	\State{$\mathbf{K}_k^\gamma=\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_k\right)^\top\left(\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_k\right)\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_k\right)^\top\right)^{-1}$}\Comment{\textbf{Map update}}
	\State{$\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_k=\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}+\mathbf{K}_k^\gamma\left(z_k-\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_k\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k-1}\right)$}
	\State{$\mathbf{P}_k^\gamma=\dfrac{1}{\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}-\mathbf{K}_k^\gamma\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}\left(\hat{x}_k\right)\right)\mathbf{P}_{k-1}^\gamma$}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:x_k^{+(i)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{x}_k,\hat{\mathbf{P}}_k\right)$}\Comment{Resampling}
	\State{$\mathbf{T}_k=\left(\mathbf{G}_k^x+\alpha_{\mathrm{RKHS},x}\mathbf{I}_N\right)^{-1}\kappa_X\left(\mathbf{x}_k^+,\mathbf{x}_k\right)$}\Comment{Embedded resampling}
	\State{$\mathbf{w}_k^+=\mathbf{T}_k\mathbf{w}_k$}
	\State{$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k|k}^+=\mathbf{T}_k\:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k|k}\:\mathbf{T}_k^\top$}
	\end{algorithmic}\caption{AKKF-SLAM}\label{alg:AKKF_SLAM}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Dependence on the origin]
	The weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ solve a regularised least-squares problem, and are not constrained to sum to one. Since $\kappa_X$ is radial, they are unchanged when the origin is translated by $t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_x}$, whereas the estimate $\hat{x}_k$ of \Cref{alg:AKKF} is moved by $\left(\mathbf{1}_N^\top\mathbf{w}_k\right)t$ instead of $t$. This estimate thus carries an error proportional to the distance between the particles and the origin, and its covariance a term growing with its square. Both dominate as soon as this distance is large compared with the dispersion of the particles.

	In \Cref{alg:AKKF_SLAM}, this dependence is removed by computing the two moments with respect to the empirical mean $\bar{x}_k$ of the particles $\mathbf{x}_k$:
	\begin{equation}
		\left\{\begin{aligned}
		\hat{x}_k&=\bar{x}_k+\sum_{i=1}^Nw_k^{(i)}\left(x_k^{(i)}-\bar{x}_k\right)\\
		\hat{\mathbf{P}}_k&=\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^N\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k|k}\right]_{ij}\left(x_k^{(i)}-\bar{x}_k\right)\left(x_k^{(j)}-\bar{x}_k\right)^\top
		\end{aligned}\right.
	\end{equation}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Estimated training inputs]
	The criterion \eqref{eq:RLS_criterion} is computed with the state estimates $\hat{x}_l$ in place of the unknown true states. The estimation errors of the filter are thus transferred to the estimated field, from which the following observations are simulated. Unlike \Cref{alg:SLAM_RBPF}, in which each set of coefficients is associated with the trajectory of its particle, \Cref{alg:AKKF_SLAM} does not account for the uncertainty of these training inputs.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Cost of AKKF-SLAM]
	In addition to the $\mathcal{O}\left(N^3\right)$ operations of \Cref{alg:AKKF}, each iteration of \Cref{alg:AKKF_SLAM} evaluates the $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}$ features at the $N$ particles, computes the variances \eqref{eq:AKKF_SLAM_variance} of the simulated observations in $\mathcal{O}\left(N\:r_{\mathrm{RFF}}^2\right)$ operations, and updates the coefficients in $\mathcal{O}\left(r_{\mathrm{RFF}}^2\right)$ operations. Its cost is thus $\mathcal{O}\left(N^3+N\:r_{\mathrm{RFF}}^2\right)$ operations per time step. Since a single set of coefficients is estimated, it only requires the storage of $\mathcal{O}\left(N^2+r_{\mathrm{RFF}}^2\right)$ values.
\end{remark}

\section{Numerical Simulations}\label{sec:SLAM_results}

\subsection{Simulation Scenario}

The two methods are compared on a scenario derived from that of \cref{sec:scenario}. The state of the vehicle, its evolution model, its speed and the time step are unchanged, as well as the covariances $\mathbf{Q}_k$ and $\mathbf{R}_k$. However, three elements differ.

First, the magnetic anomaly map is replaced by an analytical field derived from that of \cite{bib:RFF-KKF-SLAM}:
\begin{equation}
	h:x\mapsto150\left(\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\:p^{(x)}}{25}\right)+0.5\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\:p^{(y)}}{15}\right)+0.3\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\left(p^{(x)}+p^{(y)}\right)}{45}\right)\right)
\end{equation}
where $h(x)$ is expressed in nT, and the positions $p^{(x)}$ and $p^{(y)}$ in km. Its standard deviation is $123$ nT. Since \Cref{th:SLAM_assumptions} assumes a centred field, its mean over the neighbourhood of the trajectory, equal to $-0.4$ nT, is subtracted from both the field and the observations. Unlike the magnetic anomaly map, whose variations cover a wide range of spatial scales, this field only contains three spatial frequencies, and can thus be represented by a basis of moderate rank.

Second, the mower pattern is replaced by a closed circuit, which the vehicle follows several times. This circuit is a square of side $60$ km, whose corners are rounded with the turn radius of $25.5$ km, and has a length of $196$ km. The mission lasts $N_\mathrm{steps}=300$ steps, which corresponds to $3.8$ laps. Unlike the branches of the mower pattern, which are $51$ km apart, this trajectory repeatedly crosses regions that have already been observed. This is required for the field estimated during a lap to be used during the following ones.

Finally, the initial uncertainty is reduced to:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{P}_0=\begin{bmatrix}
	1000^2&0&0&0\\
	0&1000^2&0&0\\
	0&0&2^2&0\\
	0&0&0&2^2\\
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
in the units of \cref{sec:scenario}. Indeed, since the kernel is radial, translating both the trajectory and the field does not change the distribution of the observations. Without training data, the error on the initial position can therefore not be corrected, and a SLAM method can only limit the growth of the estimation error over time.

Five filters are compared, each using $N=500$ particles:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The RPF of \cref{sec:scenario} using the known field, which serves as a reference case.
	\item The AKKF of \Cref{alg:AKKF}, also using the known field. It separates the behaviour of this filter from that of the map learnt by AKKF-SLAM.
	\item Inertial navigation, in which the particles are propagated without any correction.
	\item RBPF-SLAM, presented by \Cref{alg:SLAM_RBPF}.
	\item AKKF-SLAM, as given by \Cref{alg:AKKF_SLAM}.
\end{itemize}

Both SLAM methods are given the same kernel, which is a Matérn kernel of smoothness $\nu=3/2$, variance $\sigma_h^2=125^2$ $(\text{nT})^2$ and correlation length $\ell=4$ km, on the position subspace of dimension $d_{\mathrm{pos}}=2$. It must be noted that the field is deterministic and only contains three spatial frequencies, so that \Cref{th:SLAM_assumptions} is deliberately misspecified. The correlation of $\kappa_{3/2}$ is close to one half at a distance $\ell$. The correlation length is thus chosen close to $25/6\approx4.2$ km, the distance at which the autocorrelation of the main component of the field, of wavelength $25$ km, falls to one half.

For RBPF-SLAM, the domain $\Omega$ is the smallest rectangle containing the trajectory, extended by a margin of $25$ km on each side, and $r_{\mathrm{KL}}=300$ basis functions are used. Its regularisation uses the parameters of \cref{sec:scenario}.

For AKKF-SLAM, $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}=500$ features are used, with $\alpha_{\mathrm{RKHS},x}=0.1$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{RKHS},z}=1$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{RLS}}=0.01$ and $\beta_{\mathrm{RLS}}=0.995$. Their frequencies and phases are drawn again for each Monte Carlo run. The coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{RKHS},z}$ is ten times larger than in \cite{bib:RFF-KKF-SLAM}, which reduces the gain $\mathbf{K}_k$ of the embedded correction. The kernel $\kappa_X$ is an SE kernel of unit variance and correlation length, using the Mahalanobis norm associated with $\mathbf{P}_0$. The kernel $\kappa_Z$ is an SE kernel of unit variance, whose correlation length is set at each time step to the median of the distances between the simulated observations and $z_k$, with a minimum of $\sqrt{\mathbf{R}_k}$. The AKKF using the known field shares these settings, and also computes its moments with respect to the empirical mean of the particles.

This scenario is illustrated by \Cref{fig:SLAM_scenario} and summarised by \Cref{tab:SLAM_scenario}.

Each configuration is run over $N_\mathrm{MC}=100$ Monte Carlo realisations. As in \cref{sec:scenario}, their performance is read through the estimation RMSE \eqref{eq:estimation_RMSE} and the NEES \eqref{eq:estimation_NEES}. The RMSE is also averaged over a set $E$ of time steps, which gives the $\mathrm{ARMSE}_{\hat{x}}(E)$ in the sense of \eqref{eq:kriging_ARMSE}.

\subsection{Numerical Results}

\Cref{fig:SLAM_RMSE} presents the RMSE and NEES of the five filters throughout the mission, and \Cref{tab:SLAM_results} gives their average values, along with the reconstruction error of the field.

	\centering\input{include/resultats_slam.tex}

When the field is known, the RPF corrects the initial error within the first five time steps, and its RMSE then remains around $160$ m. The AKKF using the known field corrects it more slowly, its RMSE being still of $480$ m at $k=30$, but then remains around $100$ m from $k=100$ onward. Over this period, its NEES lies between $0.04$ and $0.08$, below that of the RPF, so that this filter is conservative. Conversely, the error of the inertial solution increases throughout the mission, from $1.3$ km to $4.6$ km.

As expected, neither SLAM method reduces the initial error. During the first lap, the RMSE of RBPF-SLAM remains close to that of the inertial solution, and reaches $2.1$ km at $k=76$. It then decreases to $1.4$ km at $k=85$, when the vehicle crosses the beginning of the circuit for the second time. From this point onward, it increases much more slowly than the error of the inertial solution, and is equal to $2.2$ km at the end of the mission. However, its NEES exceeds $1$ from $k=118$, and leaves the consistency band from $k=179$. RBPF-SLAM thus becomes overconfident as the mission progresses. This may be explained by the resampling steps of \Cref{alg:SLAM_RBPF}, in which each new particle inherits the coefficients of its parent. The particles thus descend from a decreasing number of ancestors, whose maps they share, while the regularisation only restores the diversity of their states. The dispersion of the particles therefore underestimates the estimation error. This degeneracy is known to affect Rao-Blackwellised SLAM methods over time, regardless of the number of particles \cite{bib:RBPF-SLAM-consistency}.

On the other hand, the RMSE of AKKF-SLAM increases faster than that of the inertial solution during the first lap, and reaches $1.9$ km at $k=78$. It then remains between $1.8$ and $1.9$ km until the end of the mission, without any noticeable growth, and is lower than that of the inertial solution from $k=92$ onward. Its ARMSE over the whole mission is $1.78$ km, against $1.82$ km for RBPF-SLAM. However, the NEES of AKKF-SLAM lies between $10$ and $14$ after $k=100$, above the consistency band, although it does not increase over time, unlike that of RBPF-SLAM.

Since the AKKF using the known field is conservative, this overconfidence comes from the learnt map. This may be explained by the training inputs of \eqref{eq:RLS_criterion}, whose uncertainty is not accounted for by \Cref{alg:AKKF_SLAM}. The two modifications of the method of \cite{bib:RFF-KKF-SLAM} reduce this overconfidence without removing it. When the simulated observations only add the observation noise to the estimated field, the NEES over the last third of the mission increases from $11$ to $15$, and the ARMSE over the whole mission from $1.78$ to $1.92$ km. When the second term of \eqref{eq:AKKF_SLAM_variance} is kept but $\alpha_{\mathrm{RKHS},z}=0.1$, they increase to $22$ and $1.96$ km. Without both modifications, as in the original method, they reach $57$ and $2.70$ km, and the RMSE of AKKF-SLAM only becomes lower than that of the inertial solution from $k=170$. Finally, as in the original formulation, the state and the field can be represented by the same features, by using the approximated kernel $(x,x')\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x)\:\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x')^\top$ as $\kappa_X$. With $\alpha_{\mathrm{RLS}}=\mathbf{R}_k/\sigma_h^2$, so that the prior distribution $\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathbf{R}_k\:\mathbf{P}_0^\gamma\right)$ of the coefficients becomes $\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_h^2\:\mathbf{I}_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}\right)$, the NEES over the last third of the mission then decreases to $2.4$ and remains within the consistency band throughout the mission, for an ARMSE of $1.71$ km, but the field error along the trajectory increases to $95.0$ nT.

Regarding the field, both methods reduce the reconstruction error along the trajectory, from $102.5$ nT without any map to $78.9$ nT for RBPF-SLAM, and $79.8$ nT for AKKF-SLAM. \Cref{fig:SLAM_maps} presents the field reconstructed by each method at the end of the first run, along with the RMSE of the reconstruction over the $N_\mathrm{MC}$ runs. Both methods only recover the field in the neighbourhood of the circuit. Away from it, the field reconstructed by RBPF-SLAM vanishes, whereas that of AKKF-SLAM oscillates.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
	\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../../Images/Cartes reconstruites du SLAM.pdf}
	\caption{Field reconstructed by RBPF-SLAM and AKKF-SLAM at the end of the first run, with the true trajectory in black and the estimated one in blue, and RMSE of the reconstruction at each point of $\Omega$ over the $N_\mathrm{MC}$ runs.}\label{fig:SLAM_maps}
\end{figure}

These errors remain large compared with the standard deviation of the field, for two reasons. First, the field is estimated at the estimated positions of the vehicle, so that an error of about $2$ km on these positions is transferred to the reconstructed field, whose shortest wavelength is $15$ km. Second, the coefficients computed by \eqref{eq:RLS} along the trajectory estimated by the AKKF using the known field, whose ARMSE is $187$ m, still reconstruct the field with an error of $66.4$ nT. Over the rest of $\Omega$, where the field is not observed, neither method improves on the absence of map, with errors of $104.3$ nT for RBPF-SLAM and $113.4$ nT for AKKF-SLAM, against $102.7$ nT without any map. The larger error of AKKF-SLAM corresponds to the oscillations of its reconstructed field away from the circuit.

The two methods also differ in their costs. RBPF-SLAM stores $N\:r_{\mathrm{KL}}^2=4.5\times10^7$ values for the covariance matrices of its coefficients, that is $361$ MB, whereas AKKF-SLAM stores $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}^2=2.5\times10^5$ values for its single set of coefficients, in addition to its matrices of size $N\times N$, that is $8$ MB in total. Conversely, AKKF-SLAM requires $713$ Gflop per run, against $60.9$ Gflop for RBPF-SLAM. Indeed, for these values of $N$ and $r_{\mathrm{KL}}$, the $\mathcal{O}\left(N^3\right)$ operations of the AKKF outweigh the $\mathcal{O}\left(N\:r_{\mathrm{KL}}^2\right)$ operations of the $N$ Kalman corrections. Over the $1500$ s of the mission, AKKF-SLAM thus requires an average computational rate of $0.48$ Gflop/s, as much as static kriging on the coarsest grid of \Cref{tab:cout_maille}, whereas RBPF-SLAM requires $0.04$ Gflop/s. At equal computational cost, however, RBPF-SLAM becomes the more accurate method. With $N=200$ particles, AKKF-SLAM requires $71.2$ Gflop per run, close to the $60.9$ Gflop of RBPF-SLAM. Its ARMSE over the whole mission then increases from $1.78$ to $2.10$ km, against $1.82$ km for RBPF-SLAM, and its NEES over the last third of the mission from $11$ to $24$. It still keeps $63\%$ of its runs below $2$ km, against $50\%$ for RBPF-SLAM, with $3$ MB of memory against $361$ MB.

Finally, \Cref{fig:SLAM_ranks} and \Cref{tab:SLAM_ranks} compare RBPF-SLAM and AKKF-SLAM for several ranks of their bases. For $r_{\mathrm{KL}}=50$ and $100$, RBPF-SLAM diverges, with ARMSEs of $7.1$ and $7.8$ km, larger than that of the inertial solution, and field errors of $333.5$ and $188.2$ nT. This may be explained by the resolution of its basis. On $\Omega$, the shortest wavelength among the $r_{\mathrm{KL}}$ frequencies of smallest norm is $18.3$ km for $r_{\mathrm{KL}}=100$, and $13.3$ km for $r_{\mathrm{KL}}=200$, whereas the field contains a component of wavelength $15$ km. From $r_{\mathrm{KL}}=200$ onward, the ARMSE of RBPF-SLAM lies between $1.5$ and $2.1$ km, without any clear trend, and its field error between $73.4$ and $78.9$ nT. However, its cost grows quadratically with its rank, from $27.1$ Gflop per run and $161$ MB for $r_{\mathrm{KL}}=200$ to $164$ Gflop and $1$ GB for $r_{\mathrm{KL}}=500$.

In contrast, the ARMSE of AKKF-SLAM remains between $1.6$ and $1.8$ km from $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}=50$ to $1000$, and its NEES between $9$ and $16$, whereas its field error decreases from $172.5$ to $76.9$ nT. Its accuracy is thus not limited by the representation of the field. Indeed, its RMSE is already $1.41$ km at $k=1$, because of the initial error that neither SLAM method can correct, and a more accurate map can only reduce the rest of its error. The same holds for the method of \cite{bib:RFF-KKF-SLAM}, whose ARMSE remains between $2.5$ and $3.0$ km from $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}=50$ to $2000$. Since the cost of AKKF-SLAM is dominated by the $\mathcal{O}\left(N^3\right)$ operations of the AKKF, it only grows from $638$ Gflop per run for $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}=50$ to $713$ Gflop for $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}=500$, and $939$ Gflop for $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}=1000$.

Taken together, these results show that both methods limit the growth of the estimation error in the absence of training data. On this scenario, AKKF-SLAM reaches the accuracy of RBPF-SLAM with a single set of coefficients, and thus with $45$ times less memory, but requires $12$ times more operations. Moreover, the accuracy of RBPF-SLAM requires a basis that resolves the shortest wavelength of the field, and its cost grows quadratically with its rank, whereas neither the accuracy nor the cost of AKKF-SLAM varies much with the number of its features. AKKF-SLAM is also more robust, with $73\%$ of its runs ending below $2$ km against $50\%$ for RBPF-SLAM, and no divergence at any rank. However, with the settings of \Cref{tab:SLAM_scenario}, neither method provides a consistent estimate of its own uncertainty, and both remain far from the accuracy reached with the known field. In the case of AKKF-SLAM, this inconsistency comes from the learnt map, the AKKF itself being conservative when the field is known.
