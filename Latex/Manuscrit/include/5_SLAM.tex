\section{Introduction}

Throughout \cref{sec:kriging_navigation}, we assumed to have access to at least some prior knowledge of the environment in the form of a sparse and noisy set of training data. Such training points formed the basis on which we were able to reconstruct the observation function using kriging, without which no Bayesian correction could be performed.

In this chapter, we address the navigation problem described by \eqref{eq:navigation_problem} without any prior training data. This situation corresponds to Simultaneous Localisation and Mapping (SLAM), where the environment must be learnt while simultaneously estimating the vehicle's position and kinematic state. SLAM is a well-established problem in autonomous navigation. A standard solution is to use exteroceptive sensors, such as a stereo camera, LiDAR, or sonar, which provide information about the surrounding environment beyond the vehicle's immediate vicinity.

In our case however, we focus on local sensors, such as magnetometers or gravimeters, whose measurements are restricted to the field at the vehicle's current location. They consequently provide no direct information about the neighbouring environment, preventing the evaluation of the likelihood at any location other than the true one, which a Bayesian filter is precisely trying to recover.

Similarly to \cref{sec:kriging_navigation}, additional assumptions on the environment must be made in order to be able to do something. In this chapter, we assume the field to be stationary, such that it can be fully represented by the same GP on the whole of $\mathbb{R}^{d_x}$ and across time. This is formally modelled by \cref{th:SLAM_assumptions}.

\begin{assumption}[Stationary observation model]\label{th:SLAM_assumptions}
	The observation function is a time-invariant scalar GP, and its noise is an additive Gaussian white noise of variance $R_k>0$:
	\begin{equation}
		z_k=h(x_k)+n_k,\qquad n_k\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R_k)
	\end{equation}
	where the mean of the GP is known, and thus assumed to be zero without loss of generality, and its covariance kernel is a Matérn kernel $k_\nu$ with known hyperparameters, in the sense of \Cref{th:Matérn}.

	Unlike \cref{th:kriging_navigation_assumptions}, no training data are available.
\end{assumption}

This chapter is organised as follows:
\begin{itemize}
	\item\Cref{sec:RBPF_SLAM} presents an existing SLAM method based on an RBPF formulation to estimate the coefficients of the Karhunen-Loève decomposition associated with the environment.
	\item\Cref{sec:RKHS_SLAM} uses the AKKF to embed the field into an RKHS, and solve for its coefficients in this space.
	\item Finally, \cref{sec:SLAM_results} compares the two methods on a common simulation scenario.
\end{itemize}

\section{Rao-Blackwellised Mapping}\label{sec:RBPF_SLAM}

Without training data, the kriging estimators of \cref{sec:kriging} cannot be computed, and the observation function must be estimated alongside the state. However, a GP is an infinite-dimensional object, and cannot be added as such to the state of a filter.

The method proposed in \cite{bib:magnetic-SLAM-3D} addresses this issue by representing the field with a finite number of coefficients, which are then estimated jointly with the state by the RBPF of \cref{sec:particle_filtering}. This method is not a contribution of this thesis. It is presented in this section as the state-of-the-art approach to which the method proposed in \cref{sec:RKHS_SLAM} is compared.

In its original form, this method is applied to the three components of the magnetic field, modelled as the gradient of a scalar potential \cite{bib:magnetic-GP,bib:magnetic-interpolation}, and the domain is divided into hexagonal tiles, each associated with its own set of coefficients. For the sake of simplicity, it is presented here for a scalar field and a single domain.

\subsection{Reduced-Rank Field Representation}

According to \Cref{th:KL}, a GP defined on a compact set can be decomposed over the eigenfunctions of its Mercer operator. Truncating this decomposition at rank $r_{\mathrm{KL}}\in\mathbb{N}^*$ gives the finite-dimensional parametrisation of \Cref{th:KL_parametrisation}, in which the field is described by $r_{\mathrm{KL}}$ coefficients. Formally, we assume that the state of the vehicle remains within a bounded domain:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{U}\triangleq\prod_{d=1}^{d_x}[a_d,b_d]
\end{equation}
and we let $L_d\triangleq b_d-a_d$ denote its length along the $d$-th axis.

However, as noted in \Cref{th:eigenpairs}, the eigenpairs of the Mercer operator are not available in closed form in general. For a radial kernel, they can be approximated using the eigenfunctions of the Laplace operator on $\mathcal{U}$ with Dirichlet boundary conditions, which are known explicitly \cite{bib:reduced-rank}. Let $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_{r_{\mathrm{KL}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_x}$ denote the $r_{\mathrm{KL}}$ vectors of smallest norm among the frequencies:
\begin{equation}
	\pi\begin{bmatrix}
	\dfrac{n_1}{L_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{n_{d_x}}{L_{d_x}}
	\end{bmatrix}^\top,\qquad n_1,\dots,n_{d_x}\in\mathbb{N}^*
\end{equation}

The eigenpairs of the Mercer operator are then approximated by:
\begin{equation}
	\left\{\begin{aligned}
	\psi_j&:x\in\mathcal{U}\mapsto\prod_{d=1}^{d_x}\sqrt{\frac{2}{L_d}}\:\sin\left(\omega_j^{(d)}\left(x^{(d)}-a_d\right)\right)\\
	\lambda_j&=S_\nu(\omega_j)
	\end{aligned}\right.\label{eq:Laplace_basis}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_j^{(d)}$ and $x^{(d)}$ denote the $d$-th components of $\omega_j$ and $x$, and $S_\nu$ is the Fourier transform of $k_\nu$ given in \cref{sec:proof_Matérn}, also known as its spectral density.

Let $\Psi$ and $\Lambda$ be defined as in \cref{sec:efficient_kriging} from these approximated eigenpairs. Under \cref{th:SLAM_assumptions}, \Cref{th:KL_parametrisation} then gives:
\begin{equation}
	\forall x\in\mathcal{U},\:h(x)\approx\Psi(x)\:\alpha,\qquad\alpha\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda)\label{eq:SLAM_map}
\end{equation}

Furthermore, the basis functions only depend on the domain, and can thus be computed before the mission.

\begin{remark}[Choice of the domain]
	The basis functions of \eqref{eq:Laplace_basis} are equal to zero on the boundary of $\mathcal{U}$, and so is the field reconstructed from \eqref{eq:SLAM_map}. The domain must therefore be chosen larger than the region covered by the vehicle. However, the frequencies are spaced by $\pi/L_d$ along the $d$-th axis. The rank $r_{\mathrm{KL}}$ required to reach a given frequency thus increases with the size of the domain, which is why the original method divides it into tiles.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Rao-Blackwellised Estimation}

Under \cref{th:SLAM_assumptions} and the approximation \eqref{eq:SLAM_map}, the observation equation becomes:
\begin{equation}
	z_k=\Psi(x_k)\:\alpha+n_k
\end{equation}
and is thus linear in the coefficients for a given state. Assuming that the evolution noise is additive and Gaussian, the navigation problem \eqref{eq:navigation_problem} is then the system \eqref{eq:RBPF_model} with:
\begin{equation}
	\left\{\begin{array}{llll}
	x_k^n=x_k&F_k^n=0&f_k^n=f_k&Q_k^n=Q_k\\
	x_k^l=\alpha&F_k^l=I_{r_{\mathrm{KL}}}&f_k^l=0&Q_k^l=0\\
	H_k=\Psi&h_k=0&Q_k^{nl}=0&
	\end{array}\right.
\end{equation}

Since $F_k^n=0$, the propagation of the state does not depend on the coefficients, and the Kalman pre-correction \eqref{eq:RBPF_precorrection_debut} through \eqref{eq:RBPF_precorrection_fin} is omitted. Moreover, since the field does not vary over time, the coefficients are constant, and the Kalman prediction \eqref{eq:RBPF_prediction_debut} through \eqref{eq:RBPF_prediction_fin} leaves their estimate and its covariance unchanged. For the sake of simplicity, we thus write $\hat{\alpha}_k^{(i)}$ and $P_k^{\alpha(i)}$ for the estimate $\hat{x}_{k|k}^{l(i)}$ of the coefficients associated with the $i$-th particle and for its covariance $P_{k|k}^{l(i)}$. They are initialised with the prior distribution of \eqref{eq:SLAM_map}:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{\alpha}_0^{(i)}=0\quad\text{and}\quad P_0^{\alpha(i)}=\Lambda
\end{equation}

Using the transition density as proposal, as described in \eqref{eq:prior_proposal}, each iteration of the RBPF is then computed in three steps:
\begin{itemize}
	\item\textbf{Particle propagation.} The particles are propagated according to the transition density:
	\begin{equation}
		x_k^{(i)}\sim p\left(\cdot|x_{k-1}^{(i)}\right)
	\end{equation}
	\item\textbf{Weight update.} Let $\Psi_k^{(i)}\triangleq\Psi\left(x_k^{(i)}\right)$. The innovation and its covariance are:
	\begin{equation}
		\left\{\begin{aligned}
		y_k^{(i)}&=z_k-\Psi_k^{(i)}\:\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}^{(i)}\\
		S_k^{(i)}&=\Psi_k^{(i)}\:P_{k-1}^{\alpha(i)}\left(\Psi_k^{(i)}\right)^\top+R_k
		\end{aligned}\right.\label{eq:SLAM_innovation}
	\end{equation}
	and the weights are updated as in \eqref{eq:weight_update_prior}, with the likelihood:
	\begin{equation}
		z_k|\mathbf{x}_{0:k}^{(i)},\mathbf{z}_{1:k-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\Psi_k^{(i)}\:\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}^{(i)},S_k^{(i)}\right)\label{eq:SLAM_likelihood}
	\end{equation}

	Similarly to \eqref{eq:kriged_likelihood}, its covariance accounts for both the uncertainty of the estimated field and the observation noise.
	\item\textbf{Kalman correction.} The coefficients associated with each particle are corrected as:
	\begin{equation}
		\left\{\begin{aligned}
		K_k^{\alpha(i)}&=P_{k-1}^{\alpha(i)}\left(\Psi_k^{(i)}\right)^\top\left(S_k^{(i)}\right)^{-1}\\
		\hat{\alpha}_k^{(i)}&=\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}^{(i)}+K_k^{\alpha(i)}\:y_k^{(i)}\\
		P_k^{\alpha(i)}&=\left(I_{r_{\mathrm{KL}}}-K_k^{\alpha(i)}\:\Psi_k^{(i)}\right)P_{k-1}^{\alpha(i)}
		\end{aligned}\right.\label{eq:SLAM_correction}
	\end{equation}
\end{itemize}

The state and the coefficients are then estimated as in \cref{sec:particle_filtering}, by the weighted means:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{x}_k=\sum_{i=1}^Nw_k^{(i)}\:x_k^{(i)}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\alpha}_k=\sum_{i=1}^Nw_k^{(i)}\:\hat{\alpha}_k^{(i)}
\end{equation}
and the field is reconstructed as $x\mapsto\Psi(x)\:\hat{\alpha}_k$.

As for the generic PF, the particles are only resampled when the effective sample size falls below $N_{\mathrm{th}}$. In this case, the coefficients and their covariance are duplicated along with their particle before the Kalman correction. The resulting algorithm is given by \Cref{alg:SLAM_RBPF}, where $i^{(j)}$ denotes the parent index of the $j$-th resampled particle.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
	\begin{algorithmic}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:x_k^{(i)}\sim p\left(\cdot|x_{k-1}^{(i)}\right)$}\Comment{Particle propagation}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:$compute $y_k^{(i)}$ and $S_k^{(i)}$ with \eqref{eq:SLAM_innovation}}\Comment{\textbf{Innovation}}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:$compute $\tilde{p}\left(z_k|x_k^{(i)}\right)$ from \eqref{eq:SLAM_likelihood}}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:\tilde{w}_k^{(i)}=w_{k-1}^{(i)}\:\tilde{p}\left(z_k|x_k^{(i)}\right)$}\Comment{Weight update}
	\State{$\displaystyle\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:w_k^{(i)}=\left.\tilde{w}_k^{(i)}\middle/\sum_{j=1}^N\tilde{w}_k^{(j)}\right.$}\Comment{Normalisation}
	\State{$\displaystyle\hat{N}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\left.1\middle/\sum_{i=1}^N\left(w_k^{(i)}\right)^2\right.$}\Comment{Resampling criterion}
	\If{$\hat{N}_{\mathrm{eff}}<N_{\mathrm{th}}$}
	\State{$\left\{x_k^{(j)},w_k^{(j)},i^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^N\gets\operatorname{Resampling}\left(\left\{x_k^{(i)},w_k^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^N\right)$}\Comment{Resampling}
	\State{$\forall j\in[\![1,N]\!],\:\left(\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}^{(j)},P_{k-1}^{\alpha(j)}\right)\gets\left(\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}^{\left(i^{(j)}\right)},P_{k-1}^{\alpha\left(i^{(j)}\right)}\right)$}\Comment{\textbf{Map duplication}}
	\EndIf
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:$compute $\hat{\alpha}_k^{(i)}$ and $P_k^{\alpha(i)}$ with \eqref{eq:SLAM_correction}}\Comment{\textbf{Kalman correction}}
	\end{algorithmic}\caption{Rao-Blackwellised SLAM}\label{alg:SLAM_RBPF}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Cost of the Rao-Blackwellised SLAM]\label{th:SLAM_cost}
	Each particle is associated with its own coefficients and covariance matrix. Each iteration of \Cref{alg:SLAM_RBPF} thus requires $\mathcal{O}\left(N\:r_{\mathrm{KL}}^2\right)$ operations, and the storage of $\mathcal{O}\left(N\:r_{\mathrm{KL}}^2\right)$ values. Unlike kriging, these costs do not depend on the number of observations collected since the beginning of the mission.
\end{remark}

\section{The RKHS Approach}\label{sec:RKHS_SLAM}

As described by \Cref{th:SLAM_cost}, the RBPF associates each particle with its own coefficients and covariance matrix, which becomes costly when the number of particles or of basis functions is large. In this section, we propose to estimate a single set of coefficients instead, shared by all the particles. The proposed method relies on the AKKF of \Cref{alg:AKKF}.

The AKKF never evaluates the likelihood, and only requires samples of $p(z_k|x_k)$. However, under \cref{th:SLAM_assumptions}, drawing these samples requires the observation function $h$, which is unknown. Since the filtering problem is already formulated in an RKHS, it is natural to estimate $h$ in such a space as well. This section introduces random Fourier features (RFF) \cite{bib:RFF,bib:RFF-survey} to obtain a finite-dimensional approximation of this space, and recursive least squares (RLS) \cite{bib:RFF-RLS} to estimate the corresponding coefficients.

\subsection{Random Fourier Features}

Let $\mathcal{H}_{k_\nu}$ be the native RKHS of the kernel $k_\nu$. According to \Cref{th:RKHS_regularisation}, the regularised least-squares estimate of $h$ in $\mathcal{H}_{k_\nu}$ is a linear combination of the features of its training points. As for kriging, its number of coefficients is thus equal to the number of training points, which increases at each time step when the observations are collected along the trajectory. To obtain a fixed number of coefficients, the kernel is approximated by a finite number of known functions instead.

Let $p_\nu$ be the probability density obtained by normalising the spectral density of $k_\nu$:
\begin{equation}
	p_\nu:\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d_x}\mapsto\left.S_\nu(\omega)\middle/\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_x}}{S_\nu(\omega')\:d\omega'}\right.
\end{equation}

According to Bochner's theorem, already used in \cref{sec:proof_Matérn}, a continuous SPD radial kernel is the Fourier transform of a positive measure. For $k_\nu$, the density of this measure is proportional to $S_\nu$, and thus to $p_\nu$. Since $k_\nu$ is real-valued and $k_\nu(x,x)=\sigma_f^2$, this gives:
\begin{equation}
	\forall x,x'\in\mathbb{R}^{d_x},\:k_\nu(x,x')=\sigma_f^2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_x}}{\cos\left(\omega^\top(x-x')\right)p_\nu(\omega)\:d\omega}
\end{equation}

Let $\omega$ be a random variable of probability density $p_\nu$, and $b\sim\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi])$ be independent of $\omega$. We have:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
	\mathbb{E}\left[2\cos\left(\omega^\top x+b\right)\cos\left(\omega^\top x'+b\right)\right]&=\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\omega^\top(x-x')\right)\right]\\
	&\qquad+\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\omega^\top(x+x')+2b\right)\right]\\
	&=\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\omega^\top(x-x')\right)\right]
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
since the expectation of the second term with respect to $b$ is zero. The kernel can thus be written as an expectation:
\begin{equation}
	k_\nu(x,x')=\sigma_f^2\:\mathbb{E}\left[2\cos\left(\omega^\top x+b\right)\cos\left(\omega^\top x'+b\right)\right]
\end{equation}

Similarly to \Cref{th:MC_estimator}, this expectation can be approximated by a Monte Carlo estimator. Let $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}\in\mathbb{N}^*$, and let $(\omega_j,b_j)_{j\in[\![1,r_{\mathrm{RFF}}]\!]}$ be i.i.d. draws of $(\omega,b)$. In this section, $\psi_j$ and $\Psi$ denote the corresponding features:
\begin{equation}
	\psi_j:x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_x}\mapsto\sqrt{\frac{2}{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}}\:\cos\left(\omega_j^\top x+b_j\right)
\end{equation}
and:
\begin{equation}
	\Psi(x)\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}
	\psi_1(x)&\cdots&\psi_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}(x)
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

The kernel is then approximated by:
\begin{equation}
	k_\nu(x,x')\approx\sigma_f^2\:\Psi(x)\:\Psi(x')^\top
\end{equation}

Every function of the native RKHS of this approximated kernel is a linear combination of $\psi_1,\dots,\psi_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}$. The observation function is thus estimated as:
\begin{equation}
	\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_x},\:h(x)\approx\Psi(x)\:\alpha\label{eq:RFF_map}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}$. This representation has the same form as \eqref{eq:SLAM_map}. However, its basis functions are defined on the whole of $\mathbb{R}^{d_x}$, and do not depend on any domain.

\subsection{Recursive Least Squares}

The coefficients of \eqref{eq:RFF_map} are estimated from the observations collected by the vehicle. Since the true states are unknown, each observation $z_l$ is associated with the state estimate $\hat{x}_l$ of the filter at time $l$. Formally, the estimate $\hat{\alpha}_k$ of the coefficients at time $k$ is defined as the minimiser of the regularised least-squares criterion:
\begin{equation}
	J_k:\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}\mapsto\sum_{l=1}^k\beta^{k-l}\left(z_l-\Psi\left(\hat{x}_l\right)\alpha\right)^2+\beta^k\lambda_{\mathrm{RLS}}\|\alpha\|_2^2\label{eq:RLS_criterion}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{RLS}}\in\mathbb{R}_+^*$ is a regularisation coefficient, and $\beta\in(0,1]$ is a forgetting factor, which reduces the influence of the oldest observations when $\beta<1$.

Minimising \eqref{eq:RLS_criterion} at each time step would require storing every pair $\left(\hat{x}_l,z_l\right)$. \Cref{th:RLS} shows that $\hat{\alpha}_k$ can instead be computed from $\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}$ and the last pair alone, which is known as the RLS algorithm.

\begin{proposition}[Recursive least squares]\label{th:RLS}
	Let $k\in\mathbb{N}^*$. The criterion \eqref{eq:RLS_criterion} admits a unique minimiser $\hat{\alpha}_k$, which verifies:
	\begin{equation}
		\left\{\begin{aligned}
		K_k^\alpha&=P_{k-1}^\alpha\:\Psi\left(\hat{x}_k\right)^\top\left(\beta+\Psi\left(\hat{x}_k\right)P_{k-1}^\alpha\:\Psi\left(\hat{x}_k\right)^\top\right)^{-1}\\
		\hat{\alpha}_k&=\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}+K_k^\alpha\left(z_k-\Psi\left(\hat{x}_k\right)\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}\right)\\
		P_k^\alpha&=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(I_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}-K_k^\alpha\:\Psi\left(\hat{x}_k\right)\right)P_{k-1}^\alpha
		\end{aligned}\right.\label{eq:RLS}
	\end{equation}
	with:
	\begin{equation}
		\hat{\alpha}_0=0\quad\text{and}\quad P_0^\alpha=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{RLS}}}I_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}
	\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
	Writing $\Psi_l$ for $\Psi\left(\hat{x}_l\right)$, we let:
	\begin{equation}
		P_k^\alpha\triangleq\left(\sum_{l=1}^k\beta^{k-l}\:\Psi_l^\top\Psi_l+\beta^k\lambda_{\mathrm{RLS}}I_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}\right)^{-1}
	\end{equation}
	which is well defined as the inverse of the sum of an SPSD and an SPD matrix. In particular, $P_0^\alpha$ is the matrix given above. The function $J_k$ is strictly convex, and differentiating it with respect to $\alpha$ yields:
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{aligned}
		&\frac{\partial J_k}{\partial\alpha}=0\\
		\iff\quad&\sum_{l=1}^k\beta^{k-l}\:\Psi_l^\top\left(\Psi_l\:\alpha-z_l\right)+\beta^k\lambda_{\mathrm{RLS}}\:\alpha=0\\
		\iff\quad&\alpha=P_k^\alpha\sum_{l=1}^k\beta^{k-l}\:\Psi_l^\top z_l
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
	which gives the unique minimiser $\hat{\alpha}_k$. This expression also holds for $k=0$ with $\hat{\alpha}_0=0$.

	By definition, we have:
	\begin{equation}
		\left\{\begin{aligned}
		\left(P_k^\alpha\right)^{-1}&=\beta\left(P_{k-1}^\alpha\right)^{-1}+\Psi_k^\top\Psi_k\\
		\left(P_k^\alpha\right)^{-1}\hat{\alpha}_k&=\beta\left(P_{k-1}^\alpha\right)^{-1}\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}+\Psi_k^\top z_k
		\end{aligned}\right.
	\end{equation}

	The Woodbury inversion formula (see \Cref{th:Woodbury}) applied to the first equation gives:
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{aligned}
		P_k^\alpha&=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(P_{k-1}^\alpha-P_{k-1}^\alpha\:\Psi_k^\top\left(\beta+\Psi_k\:P_{k-1}^\alpha\:\Psi_k^\top\right)^{-1}\Psi_k\:P_{k-1}^\alpha\right)\\
		&=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(I_{r_{\mathrm{RFF}}}-K_k^\alpha\:\Psi_k\right)P_{k-1}^\alpha
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
	and thus:
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{aligned}
		P_k^\alpha\:\Psi_k^\top&=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(K_k^\alpha\left(\beta+\Psi_k\:P_{k-1}^\alpha\:\Psi_k^\top\right)-K_k^\alpha\:\Psi_k\:P_{k-1}^\alpha\:\Psi_k^\top\right)\\
		&=K_k^\alpha
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}

	Combining these results finally yields:
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{aligned}
		\hat{\alpha}_k&=P_k^\alpha\left(\beta\left(P_{k-1}^\alpha\right)^{-1}\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}+\Psi_k^\top z_k\right)\\
		&=P_k^\alpha\left(\left(\left(P_k^\alpha\right)^{-1}-\Psi_k^\top\Psi_k\right)\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}+\Psi_k^\top z_k\right)\\
		&=\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}+K_k^\alpha\left(z_k-\Psi_k\:\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}\right)
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\subsection{AKKF-SLAM}

Since \eqref{eq:RLS} only involves the pair $\left(\hat{x}_k,z_k\right)$, the coefficients can be updated at each time step, within the recursion of the AKKF. Each iteration of the resulting filter is then computed in four steps:
\begin{itemize}
	\item\textbf{Prediction.} The particles are propagated through the evolution model, and the embedded prediction is performed as in \Cref{alg:AKKF}.
	\item\textbf{Correction.} Since $h$ is unknown, the simulated observations are generated from the current estimate of the field:
	\begin{equation}
		\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:z_k^{(i)}=\Psi\left(x_k^{(i)}\right)\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}+n_k^{(i)},\qquad n_k^{(i)}\sim p_k^{(o)}
	\end{equation}

	The embedded correction and the state estimation are then those of \Cref{alg:AKKF}.
	\item\textbf{Map update.} The coefficients $\hat{\alpha}_k$ are computed with \eqref{eq:RLS}, from the real observation $z_k$ and the state estimate $\hat{x}_k$.
	\item\textbf{Resampling.} The new particles are drawn from $\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{x}_k,\hat{P}_k\right)$, and the embedded resampling is performed as in \Cref{alg:AKKF}.
\end{itemize}

It must be noted that the map update only relies on the real observation $z_k$, whereas the correction step uses the observations simulated from the estimated field. The resulting algorithm, referred to as AKKF-SLAM and originally proposed in \cite{bib:RFF-KKF-SLAM}, is given by \Cref{alg:AKKF_SLAM}.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
	\begin{algorithmic}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:x_k^{(i)}\sim p\left(\cdot|x_{k-1}^{+(i)}\right)$}\Comment{Particle propagation}
	\State{$D_k=\left(G_{k-1}^x+\lambda_xI_N\right)^{-1}G_{k-1}^x-I_N$}\Comment{Embedded prediction}
	\State{$\displaystyle\Sigma_{k|k-1}=\Sigma_{k-1|k-1}^++\frac{1}{N}D_k\:D_k^\top$}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:z_k^{(i)}=\Psi\left(x_k^{(i)}\right)\hat{\alpha}_{k-1}+n_k^{(i)},\:n_k^{(i)}\sim p_k^{(o)}$}\Comment{\textbf{Simulated observations}}
	\State{$K_k=\Sigma_{k|k-1}\left(G_k^z\:\Sigma_{k|k-1}+\lambda_zI_N\right)^{-1}$}\Comment{Embedded correction}
	\State{$\mathbf{w}_k=\mathbf{w}_{k-1}^++K_k\left(k_z\left(\mathbf{z}_k,z_k\right)-G_k^z\:\mathbf{w}_{k-1}^+\right)$}
	\State{$\Sigma_{k|k}=\Sigma_{k|k-1}-K_k\:G_k^z\:\Sigma_{k|k-1}$}
	\State{$\displaystyle\hat{x}_k=\sum_{i=1}^Nw_k^{(i)}\:x_k^{(i)}$}\Comment{State estimation}
	\State{$\displaystyle\hat{P}_k=\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^N\left[\Sigma_{k|k}\right]_{ij}\:x_k^{(i)}\left(x_k^{(j)}\right)^\top$}
	\State{Compute $K_k^\alpha$, $\hat{\alpha}_k$ and $P_k^\alpha$ from \eqref{eq:RLS}}\Comment{\textbf{Map update}}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:x_k^{+(i)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{x}_k,\hat{P}_k\right)$}\Comment{Resampling}
	\State{$T_k=\left(G_k^x+\lambda_xI_N\right)^{-1}k_x\left(\mathbf{x}_k^+,\mathbf{x}_k\right)$}\Comment{Embedded resampling}
	\State{$\mathbf{w}_k^+=T_k\mathbf{w}_k$}
	\State{$\Sigma_{k|k}^+=T_k\:\Sigma_{k|k}\:T_k^\top$}
	\end{algorithmic}\caption{AKKF-SLAM}\label{alg:AKKF_SLAM}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Dependence on the origin]\label{th:AKKF_origin}
	The weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ solve a regularised least-squares problem, and are not constrained to sum to one. Since $k_x$ is radial, they are unchanged when the origin is translated by $t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_x}$, whereas $\hat{x}_k$ is moved by $\left(\mathbf{1}_N^\top\mathbf{w}_k\right)t$ instead of $t$. The state estimate thus carries an error proportional to the distance between the particles and the origin, and $\hat{P}_k$ a term growing with its square. Both dominate as soon as this distance is large compared with the dispersion of the particles.

	This dependence is removed by computing the two moments with respect to the empirical mean $\bar{x}_k$ of the particles $\mathbf{x}_k$:
	\begin{equation}
		\left\{\begin{aligned}
		\hat{x}_k&=\bar{x}_k+\sum_{i=1}^Nw_k^{(i)}\left(x_k^{(i)}-\bar{x}_k\right)\\
		\hat{P}_k&=\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^N\left[\Sigma_{k|k}\right]_{ij}\left(x_k^{(i)}-\bar{x}_k\right)\left(x_k^{(j)}-\bar{x}_k\right)^\top
		\end{aligned}\right.
	\end{equation}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Estimated training inputs]
	The criterion \eqref{eq:RLS_criterion} is computed with the state estimates $\hat{x}_l$ in place of the unknown true states. The estimation errors of the filter are thus transferred to the estimated field, from which the following observations are simulated. Unlike \Cref{alg:SLAM_RBPF}, in which each set of coefficients is associated with the trajectory of its particle, \Cref{alg:AKKF_SLAM} does not account for the uncertainty of these training inputs.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Cost of AKKF-SLAM]
	In addition to the $\mathcal{O}\left(N^3\right)$ operations of \Cref{alg:AKKF}, each iteration of \Cref{alg:AKKF_SLAM} evaluates the $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}$ features at the $N$ particles, and updates the coefficients in $\mathcal{O}\left(r_{\mathrm{RFF}}^2\right)$ operations. Its cost is thus $\mathcal{O}\left(N^3+N\:r_{\mathrm{RFF}}+r_{\mathrm{RFF}}^2\right)$ operations per time step. Since a single set of coefficients is estimated, it only requires the storage of $\mathcal{O}\left(N^2+r_{\mathrm{RFF}}^2\right)$ values.
\end{remark}

\section{Numerical Simulations}\label{sec:SLAM_results}

\subsection{Simulation Scenario}

The two methods are compared on a scenario derived from that of \cref{sec:scenario}. The state of the vehicle, its evolution model, its speed and the time step are unchanged, as well as the covariances $Q_k$ and $R_k$. However, three elements differ.

First, the magnetic anomaly map is replaced by the analytical field of \cite{bib:RFF-KKF-SLAM}, scaled to the dimensions of the scenario:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
	h:x\mapsto151.5&\left(\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\:p^{(x)}}{25}\right)+0.5\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\:p^{(y)}}{14.58}\right)\right.\\
	&\qquad\left.+0.3\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\left(p^{(x)}+p^{(y)}\right)}{43.75}\right)\right)
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $h(x)$ is expressed in nT, and the positions $p^{(x)}$ and $p^{(y)}$ in km. Its standard deviation is $124$ nT. Unlike the magnetic anomaly map, whose variations cover a wide range of spatial scales, this field only contains three spatial frequencies, and can thus be represented by a basis of moderate rank.

Second, the mower pattern is replaced by a closed circuit, which the vehicle follows several times. This circuit is a square of side $60$ km, whose corners are rounded with the turn radius of $25.5$ km, and has a length of $196$ km. The mission lasts $n_\mathrm{steps}=300$ steps, which corresponds to $3.8$ laps. Unlike the branches of the mower pattern, which are $51$ km apart, this trajectory repeatedly crosses regions that have already been observed. This is required for the field estimated during a lap to be used during the following ones.

Finally, the initial uncertainty is reduced to:
\begin{equation}
	P_0=\begin{bmatrix}
	1000^2&0&0&0\\
	0&1000^2&0&0\\
	0&0&2^2&0\\
	0&0&0&2^2\\
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
in the units of \cref{sec:scenario}. Indeed, since the kernel is radial, translating both the trajectory and the field does not change the distribution of the observations. Without training data, the error on the initial position can therefore not be corrected, and a SLAM method can only limit the growth of the estimation error over time.

Five filters are compared, each using $N=500$ particles:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The RPF of \cref{sec:scenario} using the known field, which serves as a reference case.
	\item The same RPF using static kriging on a training set of resolution $\Delta_g=3.5$ km, built as in \cref{sec:scenario}. It corresponds to the method of \cref{sec:kriging_PF}, when a prior survey of the field is available.
	\item The inertial navigation, in which the particles are propagated without any correction.
	\item The RBPF of \Cref{alg:SLAM_RBPF}.
	\item AKKF-SLAM, as given by \Cref{alg:AKKF_SLAM}.
\end{itemize}

Both SLAM methods are given the same kernel, which is a Matérn kernel of smoothness $\nu=3/2$, variance $\sigma_f^2=124^2$ $(\text{nT})^2$ and correlation length $l=4$ km. Since the field only depends on the position of the vehicle, their basis functions are defined on the position subspace, of dimension $d_{\mathrm{pos}}=2$, and evaluated at $\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:x$.

For the RBPF, the domain $\mathcal{U}$ is the smallest rectangle containing the trajectory, extended by a margin of $25$ km on each side, and $r_{\mathrm{KL}}=300$ basis functions are used. Its particles are regularised as in \Cref{alg:RPF}, with the parameters of \cref{sec:scenario}. Moreover, the variance of the field that is not represented by the truncated basis is added to $R_k$ in \eqref{eq:SLAM_innovation}.

For AKKF-SLAM, $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}=500$ features are used, with $\lambda_x=\lambda_z=0.1$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{RLS}}=0.01$ and $\beta=0.995$. The kernel $k_x$ is an SE kernel of unit variance and correlation length, using the Mahalanobis norm associated with $P_0$. The kernel $k_z$ is an SE kernel of unit variance, whose correlation length is set at each time step to the median of the distances between the simulated observations and $z_k$, with a minimum of $\sqrt{R_k}$. Since the positions are large compared with the dispersion of the particles, the state estimate and its covariance are computed with respect to the empirical mean of the particles, as described in \Cref{th:AKKF_origin}.

This scenario is illustrated by \Cref{fig:SLAM_scenario} and summarised by \Cref{tab:SLAM_scenario}.

Each filter is run over $n_\mathrm{MC}=100$ Monte Carlo realisations. As in \cref{sec:scenario}, their performance is read through the estimation RMSE \eqref{eq:estimation_RMSE} and the NEES. \begin{table}[!htb]
	\centering\input{include/resultats_slam.tex}
	\caption{Estimation ARMSE of the five filters over three successive parts of the mission, NEES averaged over the last one, and reconstruction ARMSE of the field along the trajectory. For the inertial navigation, the field is reconstructed as zero.}\label{tab:SLAM_results}

When a map is available, whether known or kriged from a prior survey, the RPF corrects the initial error within the first ten time steps, and its RMSE then remains around $200$ m. Conversely, the error of the inertial navigation increases throughout the mission, from $1.3$ km to $4.6$ km.

As expected, neither SLAM method reduces the initial error. During the first lap, the RMSE of the RBPF increases slightly faster than that of the inertial navigation, and reaches $2.0$ km at $k=76$. It then decreases to $1.4$ km at $k=85$, when the vehicle crosses the beginning of the circuit for the second time. From this point onward, it increases much more slowly than the error of the inertial navigation, and is equal to $2.0$ km at the end of the mission. However, its NEES exceeds $1$ from $k=95$, and leaves the consistency band from $k=142$. The RBPF thus becomes overconfident as the mission progresses.

On the other hand, the RMSE of AKKF-SLAM increases faster than that of the inertial navigation at the beginning of the mission, and reaches $3.1$ km at $k=38$. It then remains between $2.2$ and $2.9$ km until the end of the mission, without any noticeable growth, and becomes lower than that of the inertial navigation from $k=169$. However, it remains higher than that of the RBPF, and the NEES of AKKF-SLAM lies around $50$, far above the consistency band.

Regarding the field, both methods reduce the reconstruction error along the trajectory, from $103.7$ nT without any map to $78.4$ nT for the RBPF, and $89.4$ nT for AKKF-SLAM. These errors remain large compared with the standard deviation of the field. This may be explained by the position error. Indeed, the field is estimated at the estimated positions of the vehicle, so that an error of about $2$ km on these positions is transferred to the reconstructed field, whose shortest wavelength is $14.58$ km. Outside this region, the field is not observed, and neither method improves on the absence of map.

Finally, the two methods differ in their memory requirements. The RBPF stores $N\:r_{\mathrm{KL}}^2=4.5\times10^7$ values for the covariance matrices of its coefficients, whereas AKKF-SLAM stores $r_{\mathrm{RFF}}^2=2.5\times10^5$ values for its single set of coefficients, in addition to its matrices of size $N\times N$.

Taken together, these results show that both methods limit the growth of the estimation error in the absence of training data. On this scenario, the RBPF is the more accurate of the two, while AKKF-SLAM bounds its error with a single set of coefficients, at a lower memory cost. However, neither method provides a consistent estimate of its own uncertainty, and both remain far from the accuracy reached when a prior survey is available.
