\subsection{Non-Stationary Observation Field}

\textbf{TODO: à vérifier — section entièrement rédigée par Claude.}

The estimators of \cref{sec:kriging} assume a single kernel over the whole index set, and
\cref{th:kriging_navigation_assumptions} inherits that assumption. A physical field rarely
honours it. A magnetic anomaly map is rough where the basement is shallow and smooth where a
sedimentary cover buries it, so a correlation length fitted over the whole map is an average
that describes no part of it, too long where the field varies quickly and too short where it
does not. The estimator then either smooths away structure it could have recovered, or credits
noise with a structure it does not have, and in both cases the covariance it reports is wrong
in the direction that matters, since a filter reads that covariance as its likelihood width.

A second observation makes this tractable rather than merely regrettable. The filter never
queries the whole map. At any step the particles occupy a region whose extent is set by the
covariance the filter itself carries, a few kilometres once the track is acquired, and the
estimator is asked for values inside that region alone. The stationarity assumption therefore
only needs to hold locally, over a neighbourhood the filter delimits at no cost, since it
computes the corresponding covariance anyway.

\subsection{Kriging Subsampling}

\textbf{TODO: à vérifier — section entièrement rédigée par Claude.}

Adaptive kriging exploits that remark by fitting and solving on a window rather than on the
whole survey. At each step the window is the disc centred on the predicted position whose
radius is the semi-major axis of the ellipse at three standard deviations, scaled by a factor
$\alpha_W$ left to the designer, and the samples it contains form the training set of an
ordinary kriging whose hyperparameters are re-estimated on them.

Two bounds are imposed on the number of samples retained, and they guard against opposite
failures. The floor $n_{\min}$ prevents a filter that has become overconfident from kriging on
two points, a configuration in which the estimate is dominated by the estimated mean and its
covariance is meaningless. The ceiling $n_{\max}$ addresses the converse. Since the radius is
proportional to the declared uncertainty, a cloud still fifteen kilometres wide calls for a disc
of ninety kilometres, that is, for the entire survey, and adaptive kriging then becomes an
ordinary kriging that pays a full factorisation at every step and recovers nothing of what it
was meant to save. Bounding the count rather than the radius keeps the selection meaningful
whether the samples are dense or sparse.

The cost per step follows from \cref{th:kriging_mean_cost} with $\tilde{n}$ replaced by the
window size $\tilde{n}_\mathrm{w}$, one factorisation in
$\mathcal{O}\left(\tilde{n}_\mathrm{w}^3\right)$ shared by the particles of the step, and one
hyperparameter fit. The saving is therefore cubic in the ratio of the window to the survey, and
it is bought at the price of a fit that the static estimator performs once for the whole
mission.

Adaptive kriging is asked one question. Whether restricting the training set to a window costs
anything in accuracy, and what it saves.

It costs nothing. The two are indistinguishable at acquisition and the windowed estimator is
some thirteen per cent better at the end, which is within the dispersion between survey draws
and should not be read as an improvement. What matters is the last column. \textbf{The window
holds fifty-three samples where the static estimator reads three thousand one hundred and
thirty-six}, and the accuracy is the same.

The saving that follows from \cref{th:kriging_mean_cost} is not linear in that ratio. Per step
the estimator pays one factorisation in
$\mathcal{O}\left(\tilde{n}_\mathrm{w}^3\right)$ and, for each of the $N$ particles, a
triangular solve in $\mathcal{O}\left(\tilde{n}_\mathrm{w}^2\right)$, the second term dominating
since $N$ far exceeds the window. Summed over the mission, adaptive kriging spends
$14.4$ Gflop against $7386$ for the static estimator evaluated exactly, a factor of about five
hundred.

\begin{remark}[Tabulation]\label{th:tabulation}
	The static estimator admits a precomputation the adaptive one does not. Its mean and
	variance being fixed functions of the position once the hyperparameters are fitted, they can
	be evaluated once on a grid covering the flight corridor and read by interpolation
	thereafter, which costs $4659$ Gflop once and nothing per step. That is why it appears the
	faster of the two on a wall clock.

	The precomputation presumes the corridor to be known in advance, which is what map-aided
	navigation is trying to establish. Where it is not, the tabulation is unavailable and the
	comparison is the one above.
\end{remark}

\Cref{fig:fenetre} shows why the window is so small. Its radius follows the covariance the
filter declares, which collapses once the track is acquired, and from the thirtieth step
onwards the disc contains fewer samples than the floor $n_{\min}$ admits. \textbf{For the whole
tracking phase it is the floor, and not the radius, that sets the window.} The dilation factor
$\alpha_W$ is therefore inert over that phase, which a sweep confirms, and $n_{\min}$ is not a
guard against a degenerate case but the operating point itself.

Two parameters define that window, and \cref{tab:fenetrage} reports what each one
buys. Neither buys accuracy. \textbf{Over a window grown from thirty-four samples to
eighty-one, the convergence rate does not move at all and the final error stays within six per
cent}, while the cost follows the window throughout — a factor of six on the queries, quadratic
in it, and fourteen on the factorisations, cubic.

The dilation factor is the more inert of the two, and \cref{fig:fenetre} says why: over the
tracking phase the disc it scales holds fewer samples than the floor admits, so it has nothing
left to act on. The floor is the exception, and only during acquisition — at fifteen samples the
median error at the thirtieth step rises by a fifth, the window being then too small to
constrain an estimate whose cloud is still wide, and from twenty-five onwards it is flat.
\textbf{Both are therefore taken at the cheap end of a plateau}, the dilation freely, the floor
with the acquisition phase in mind.

The ceiling $n_{\max}$ and the size of the subsample used for the local hyperparameter fit were
swept in the same way and are flat as well, the ceiling never binding above two thousand:
two thousand and six thousand four hundred return the same figures.


\section{Clustered Particle Filtering}\label{sec:CAK_PF}

\subsection{Clustering Methods}

\textbf{TODO: à vérifier — section entièrement rédigée par Claude.}

A window centred on the predicted position presumes that the cloud has one. It does not while
the track is being acquired. A magnetic reading is compatible with every position carrying the
same field value, so the posterior is multimodal by nature, and the mean of a cloud spread over
several distant modes lies between them, in a region no particle occupies. The window is then
centred on nothing, its radius is set by the spread between modes rather than by the uncertainty
within one, and the estimator is asked for values far from the samples it has selected.

Partitioning the cloud repairs this, provided the partition is found rather than assumed. The
number of modes is precisely what is unknown, and it changes from step to step as the ambiguity
resolves, which rules out the methods that take it as an input. Mean shift takes a bandwidth
instead, which is a length rather than a count, and returns as many modes as the cloud actually
has. Each seed walks towards the barycentre of the weighted particles lying within one bandwidth
of it, and the walk is iterated to convergence, two seeds arriving within half a bandwidth of
one another being counted as one mode. The bandwidth thus expresses how far apart two positions
must be before they count as competing explanations, which is a quantity the designer can reason
about, unlike a number of modes.

\subsection{The CAK-PF Algorithm}

\textbf{TODO: à vérifier — section entièrement rédigée par Claude.}

Clustered adaptive kriging applies the windowing of \cref{sec:AK_PF} once per mode. The position
block of the cloud is partitioned by mean shift, each mode receives the window its own centre and
covariance dictate, and the hyperparameters are re-estimated on each window, so that a cloud
straddling a rough region and a smooth one no longer averages the two into a kernel that fits
neither.

The query points are attached to the nearest centre rather than inheriting the labels of the
particles. The distinction is immaterial for the filters that query the cloud they propagated,
and it is not for the auxiliary filter, which queries the transition means before resampling and
therefore asks for values at points that are not those the partition was computed from. A centre
is a location to which both sets can be referred, whereas a label belongs to one of them.

The cost is the sum over modes of the terms of \cref{th:kriging_mean_cost}, one factorisation
and one fit per mode. Clustering therefore trades a single large factorisation for several small
ones, which is favourable as soon as the modes are genuinely separated, the cubic cost falling
faster than the count of windows grows. It is unfavourable when the cloud is unimodal, since the
mean shift is then paid for a partition of one, and the method degenerates by construction into
the adaptive kriging of \cref{sec:AK_PF} in that case.

What it makes cheaper is the whole of it, by a factor of about thirteen, and the two columns
say why. Plain adaptive kriging factorises one window of $\tilde{n}_\mathrm{w}$ samples and
solves all $N$ particles against it. Clustered adaptive kriging factorises one window per mode,
which costs $\sum_c n_c^3$ instead of $\left(\sum_c n_c\right)^3$, and \textbf{each mode solves
only the particles that belong to it}, so the dominant term is divided twice over. Measured, the
factor is three on the factorisations and twenty-one on the queries.

\begin{remark}[Where the saving is made]\label{th:cak_acquisition}
	\Cref{fig:modes} shows the mean shift returning fifty-four modes at the first step and one
	from the sixteenth onwards, after which clustered adaptive kriging degenerates by
	construction into the plain windowed estimator. The whole of the saving is therefore made
	during the acquisition phase, which is also where the single window of \cref{sec:AK_PF}
	swells to several hundred samples.

	The mean shift is nevertheless paid at every step, including the hundred and thirty-four on
	which it returns one mode. It accounts for fourteen per cent of the clustered estimator's
	cost, and that share is counted in \cref{tab:cout_CAK}.
\end{remark}

	\centering\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{../../Images/Modes au cours du vol.pdf}
	\caption{Modes returned by the mean shift along the flight. The dashed line marks the first half-turn.}\label{fig:modes}

The scenario of \cref{sec:scenario} exercises the clustering over the first tenth of its flight
alone, its initial uncertainty being modest enough that a single branch resolves the ambiguity.
\textbf{A less precise survey sustains it much longer.} At a pitch of $5.50$ km and an initial
deviation of twelve kilometres, seventy-two per cent of the steps carry more than one mode
against eleven here, at an unchanged convergence rate, and the clustered estimator keeps the
same advantage in cost and the same absence of one in accuracy. The regime in which clustering
was conceived is therefore reached by degrading the map, not by changing the filter.

\begin{remark}[Wall clock]\label{th:temps_mur}
	Clustered adaptive kriging runs some two and a half times slower than the plain windowed
	estimator on a wall clock while consuming thirteen times less arithmetic. Its mean shift is
	an interpreted loop over sixty seeds where the kriging descends into optimised linear
	algebra, and accounts for fourteen per cent of the operations and two hundred and thirty per
	cent of the time. The measurements of this chapter are therefore reported as operation
	counts, which are reproducible, and not as durations, which are not.
\end{remark}
