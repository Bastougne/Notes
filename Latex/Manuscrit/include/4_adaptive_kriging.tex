\section{Adaptive Kriging}\label{sec:AK_PF}

In this section, we propose to use training data subsampling, \textit{i.e.} selecting a subset $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$ of size $\tilde{N}^r$ of the training points and only using them to perform the regression, as a way to solve the computational issues raised above. Since the computational cost of kriging scales cubically with the number of training points, quadratically for memory, discarding half the data reduces the computational load by a factor of $8$ and requires $4$ times less storage.

However, this method comes at a cost. In the following, we describe two subsampling strategies, each requiring to perform a subsampling step at each time step. This means that no precomputation can be done before the mission, and in particular that inverting $G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)\in\mathcal{S}_{\tilde{N}^r\:d_z}^{++}(\mathbb{R})$ and $C^\top G(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r)^{-1}C\in\mathcal{S}_{d_z}^{++}(\mathbb{R})$ must be done in real time.

\subsection{Random Subsampling}

The simplest idea to reduce the number of training points is to let $p\in[0,1]$, and draw $s^{(i)}$ from a Bernoulli distribution of success probability $p$. Any training sample $\tilde{x}_i$ for which the corresponding $s^{(i)}$ is a success is retained to form $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^r$, while the others are discarded.

By linearity of the expectation, the number of retained data is, on average:
\begin{equation}
	\tilde{N}^r=p\times\tilde{N}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Adaptive Subsampling Window}

The above results show that the computational limits described in \cref{sec:static_results} are not the only reasons to forgo using static kriging-based navigation algorithms. Indeed, the estimators introduced in \cref{sec:kriging} assume the use of a single kernel over the entire domain, and \cref{th:kriging_navigation_assumptions} inherits that hypothesis. In \cref{sec:HP_estimation}, we estimated the hyperparameters of the kernel once before the mission, meaning that they are assumed to be independent of the inputs:
\begin{equation}
	k_{3/2}:x,x'\mapsto\sigma_f^2\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{3}\:\|x-x'\|_2}{l}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}\:\|x-x'\|_2}{l}\right)
\end{equation}

The considered kernel is thus radial, and the resulting GP is therefore stationary. This is unrealistic for a physical field, especially at large spatial scales. However, stationarity cannot be simply dropped, as the hyperparameters of such a kernel would have to be fitted at every point of the domain, where a single sample is available. We therefore assume the field to be non-stationary, but its hyperparameters to vary over a scale much larger than the grid resolution of the training data. Under this hypothesis, the hyperparameters must be estimated over a region large enough to provide sufficient data for accurate estimation, while remaining small enough to avoid averaging them over distances on which they vary significantly.

In this section, we propose an adaptive subsampling strategy to optimise the kriging performance of the filter according to four criteria:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The retained training points must cover an area large enough to avoid extrapolation at the particle locations.
	\item A sufficient number of training points must be retained to ensure that the hyperparameters are accurately estimated.
	\item The retained training points must be confined to a sufficiently small region for the hyperparameters to be approximately constant within this region.
	\item Finally, their number must remain small enough to ensure that the filter can be computed within real-time constraints.
\end{itemize}

The first constraint naturally gives the shape of retained subset. Let $\hat{x}_{k|k-1}$ be the predicted estimation of the filter at time $k$:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{x}_{k|k-1}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^Nw_{k-1}^{(i)}\:x_k^{(i)}\approx\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|\mathbf{z}_{1:k-1})}[x_k]
\end{equation}
and $\hat{P}_{k|k-1}$ denote the corresponding covariance matrix:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{P}_{k|k-1}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^Nw_{k-1}^{(i)}\left(x_k^{(i)}-\hat{x}_{k|k-1}\right)\left(x_k^{(i)}-\hat{x}_{k|k-1}\right)^\top
\end{equation}

These two quantities can be computed after the prediction step at time $k$, but before the following weight update, and represent a Gaussian approximation of the particle set at time $k$ before kriging the environment.

In this approximation, each particle has a $99\%$ chance to verify:
\begin{equation}
	\left\|\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:\hat{x}_{k|k-1}-\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:x_k\right\|_{\hat{P}_{\mathrm{pos},k|k-1}}^2\leq\chi_{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}^2(99\%)
\end{equation}
where $d_{\mathrm{pos}}$ is the dimension of the position subspace, $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{pos}}$ is the covariance matrix associated with the projection of the predicted state onto this subspace:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{P}_{\mathrm{pos},k|k-1}\triangleq\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:\hat{P}_{k|k-1}\:\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}^\top
\end{equation}
and $\chi_{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}^2(99\%)$ is the $99\%$ quantile of a $\chi^2$ distribution with $d_{\mathrm{pos}}$ degrees of freedom. This means that the particles are likely to be contained in an ellipsoid centred on $\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:\hat{x}_{k|k-1}$, and whose shape is given by the eigenvectors of $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{pos},k|k-1}$.

However, this geometry is tied to the particle set, and does not directly translate into the physical position space. Furthermore, when $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{pos},k|k-1}$ is highly anisotropic, the resulting ellipsoid may provide insufficient coverage along directions of lower uncertainty, leading to extrapolation. To solve this issue, we use the Rayleigh quotient \cite{bib:matrix-cookbook} to convert the Mahalanobis inequality into a Euclidean one:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
	\left\|\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:\hat{x}_{k|k-1}-\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:x_k\right\|_{\hat{P}_{\mathrm{pos},k|k-1}}^2&\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(\hat{P}_{\mathrm{pos},k|k-1}\right)\left\|\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:\hat{x}_{k|k-1}-\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:x_k\right\|_2^2\\
	&\leq\chi_{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}^2(99\%)\:\lambda_{\max}\left(\hat{P}_{\mathrm{pos},k|k-1}\right)
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The first subsampling criterion is thus satisfied by retaining training points within a ball of radius:
\begin{equation}
	r_{\mathrm{cov},k}\triangleq\alpha_{\mathrm{cov}}\sqrt{\chi_{d_{\mathrm{pos}}}^2(99\%)}\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}\left(\hat{P}_{\mathrm{pos},k|k-1}\right)}
\end{equation}
and centred on $\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:\hat{x}_{k|k-1}$, where $\alpha_{\mathrm{cov}}\geq1$ is a dilation factor, introduced to provide a margin around the particle set and reduce the risk of extrapolation at its boundaries.

However, when the particle set collapses on a small region of space, the ball may not contain enough training points to satisfy the second criterion. We thus introduce a second radius to ensure that at least $\tilde{n}_{\min}$ training points are retained:
\begin{equation}
	r_{\min,k}\triangleq\min_{r\in\mathbb{R}^+}\left(\operatorname{Card}\left\{i\in[\![1,\tilde{n}]\!]\:\middle|\:\left\|\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:\hat{x}_{k|k-1}-\tilde{x}_i\right\|_2\leq r\right\}\geq \tilde{n}_{\min}\right)
\end{equation}

The retained training set is thus defined at each time step $k$ as:
\begin{equation}
	\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k^r\triangleq\left\{\tilde{x}_i\in\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\:\middle|\:\left\|\Pi_{\mathrm{pos}}\:\hat{x}_{k|k-1}-\tilde{x}_i\right\|_2\leq\max\left(r_{\mathrm{cov},k},r_{\min,k}\right)\right\}
\end{equation}

In particular, since the navigation estimate varies slowly between two consecutive time steps, the retained training set is likely to remain similar. The hyperparameters estimated at time $k-1$ can thus be used as initial values for the estimation at time $k$, reducing the number of iterations required for convergence of the hyperparameter estimation algorithm.

	\begin{algorithmic}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:x_k^{(i)}\sim q\left(\cdot|x_{k-1}^{(i)},z_k\right)$}\Comment{Particle propagation}
	\State{$\forall i\in[\![1,N]\!],\:\tilde{w}_k^{(i)}=w_{k-1}^{(i)}\:\dfrac{\tilde{p}\left(z_k|x_k^{(i)}\right)\:\tilde{p}\left(x_k^{(i)}|x_{k-1}^{(i)}\right)}{\tilde{q}\left(x_k^{(i)}|\mathbf{x}_{0:k-1}^{(i)},\mathbf{z}_{1:k}\right)}$}\Comment{Weight update}

\subsection{Clustering Methods}

A window centred on the predicted position presumes that the cloud has one. It does not while
the track is being acquired. A magnetic reading is compatible with every position carrying the
same field value, so the posterior is multimodal by nature, and the mean of a cloud spread over
several distant modes lies between them, in a region no particle occupies. The window is then
centred on nothing, its radius is set by the spread between modes rather than by the uncertainty
within one, and the estimator is asked for values far from the samples it has selected.

Partitioning the cloud repairs this, provided the partition is found rather than assumed. The
number of modes is precisely what is unknown, and it changes from step to step as the ambiguity
resolves, which rules out the methods that take it as an input. Mean shift takes a bandwidth
instead, which is a length rather than a count, and returns as many modes as the cloud actually
has. Each seed walks towards the barycentre of the weighted particles lying within one bandwidth
of it, and the walk is iterated to convergence, two seeds arriving within half a bandwidth of
one another being counted as one mode. The bandwidth thus expresses how far apart two positions
must be before they count as competing explanations, which is a quantity the designer can reason
about, unlike a number of modes.

\subsection{The CAK-PF Algorithm}

\textbf{TODO: à vérifier — section entièrement rédigée par Claude.}

Clustered adaptive kriging applies the windowing of \cref{sec:AK_PF} once per mode. The position
block of the cloud is partitioned by mean shift, each mode receives the window its own centre and
covariance dictate, and the hyperparameters are re-estimated on each window, so that a cloud
straddling a rough region and a smooth one no longer averages the two into a kernel that fits
neither.

The query points are attached to the nearest centre rather than inheriting the labels of the
particles. The distinction is immaterial for the filters that query the cloud they propagated,
and it is not for the auxiliary filter, which queries the transition means before resampling and
therefore asks for values at points that are not those the partition was computed from. A centre
is a location to which both sets can be referred, whereas a label belongs to one of them.

The cost is the sum over modes of the terms of \cref{th:kriging_mean_cost}, one factorisation
and one fit per mode. Clustering therefore trades a single large factorisation for several small
ones, which is favourable as soon as the modes are genuinely separated, the cubic cost falling
faster than the count of windows grows. It is unfavourable when the cloud is unimodal, since the
mean shift is then paid for a partition of one, and the method degenerates by construction into
the adaptive kriging of \cref{sec:AK_PF} in that case.

What it makes cheaper is the whole of it, by a factor of about thirteen, and the two columns

\begin{remark}[Wall clock]\label{th:temps_mur}
	Clustered adaptive kriging runs some two and a half times slower than the plain windowed
	estimator on a wall clock while consuming thirteen times less arithmetic. Its mean shift is
	an interpreted loop over sixty seeds where the kriging descends into optimised linear
	algebra, and accounts for fourteen per cent of the operations and two hundred and thirty per
	cent of the time. The measurements of this chapter are therefore reported as operation
	counts, which are reproducible, and not as durations, which are not.
\end{remark}
